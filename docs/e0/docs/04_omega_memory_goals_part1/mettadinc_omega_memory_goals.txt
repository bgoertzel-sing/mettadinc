                                               Abstract
      An Omega agent makes memory decisions every cycle. It recalls notes from a journal into
      a working memory of sixty items, chooses about fifteen hundred tokens of them for the
      language model, admits a few conclusions from its reasoner, closes and ages open questions,
      and lets a goal engine propose follow-ups from what remains open; the planned runtime
      adds explicit decisions about what to keep resident, what to retain and what to admit as
      evidence. Each decision keeps some distinctions and drops others, and each is right or wrong
      only relative to the queries that later read memory: the reply writer, the goal engine, the
      reasoner, the agent’s own commitments and anchors. MeTTaDinC states such decisions
      as representations of declared machines and checks, for each declared consumer, whether
      the representation still answers its queries now, after updates and in all future behavior,
      returning a replayable witness when it does not. This guide applies that to Omega’s memory
      and goals. It proposes an architecture: a library of declarations kept beside the stores, a
      registry of consumers with custody rules, a checker that runs in the background, a certificate
      store keyed by interface digests, receipts attached to context blocks and goal suggestions,
      and a small interface through which the agent can ask about its own memory. Eight patterns
      are worked out. Two failures reported by the first live review of the goal engine, stale items
      that stayed open and paraphrases that repeated, reappear as canonical witnesses in a model
      of the engine: closing items by what a recall window shows reopens them as replies age out
      of the window, and deduplication by person and topic suggests the same question twice.
      Policies for choosing the context block are compared consumer by consumer. Consolidation
      of evidence is checked against a contradiction probe, a dependence-aware evidence count and
      an anchor’s regeneration cues, reproducing the failure modes of GOLEM-Iter’s migration
      use case and the smallest memory that serves all three. Task checkpoints without read
      sets are shown to be adequate today and wrong after a withdrawal. Goals are treated as
      observers on memory: each goal set determines the coarsest memory that serves it, adding
      a goal resumes refinement instead of restarting it, retiring one licenses consolidation, and a
      proposed change to a goal is sorted into renaming, refinement, coarsening, migration, failed
      migration or change of content by checks that keep apart the information a goal’s answers
      carry and the decisions they stand for, read through declared and protected decoders; a
      negated goal carries exactly the information of the original and is caught as a change of
      content. Conflicts between goals and a prohibition are found as shortest runs; MetaMo’s
      motivational principles are measured on a finite model shaped after its operational equations;
      and a monitor on the goal engine’s stale suggestions gives an anytime-valid alarm.


How to read this guide
Section 1 says what the guide is for. Section 2 gives the background a reader needs from
five places: the live WMTM system, the planned OCO/2 runtime, the goal machinery of
GoalChainer, GOLEM-Iter and MetaMo, MeTTaDinC itself, and the d-calculus results the
guide puts to work. Section 3 describes the architecture of the MeTTaDinC layer. Sections 4–11
are the eight patterns; each opens with the problem as it appears in Omega, gives the module,

interface the agent itself uses. Section 13 is the rollout plan, and Section 14 the limits and open

reproduction notes.
   A reader who wants the practical content first can read Section 3, then the opening and

terms restate a technical result without notation. References to “the specification” are to the
MeTTaDinC specification draft [1], and to “the worked examples” to its companion [3].

1     Introduction
The distinction calculus started from a simple question about minds: which distinctions does a
system keep, which does it let go, and relative to whom is that the right choice? A memory is a
standing answer to that question. So is a summary, a cache, a context window, a consolidation
rule, and, one level up, a goal, which singles out the distinctions the system is committed to
acting on. Omega agents now answer the question many times a minute, in code that is still
being revised as its builders learn what goes wrong. This guide is about giving those answers a
form the agent can check, and then letting the agent use the checks to reason about its own
memory and goals.
    The occasion is concrete. The WMTM layer of the Omega agent on ProtoCosmo2 recalls
notes into a working memory, chooses what the language model sees, admits conclusions from
a reasoner, and runs a goal engine over the open questions it remembers [4]. The first live
review of its goal suggestions failed, in part for reasons that are, in hindsight, statements about
distinctions: old replies were missed, so settled questions stayed open, and paraphrases of
one question were suggested again. A planned reimplementation inside the Omega runtime
introduces typed records, three separate decisions about residency, retention and admission, task
checkpoints and goal contracts [5]. GOLEM-Iter adds a promotion gate that must decide whether
a new memory policy endangers a protected goal [7]. MetaMo describes how motivational state
should evolve [10, 11]. Several of these documents ask, in their own words, for claims of the
kind MeTTaDinC was designed to state: that a compact view keeps what its users need, now
and after the next update; that a migration commutes with repair; that a goal change preserves
the answers it claims to preserve.

1.1    What this guide provides
The guide proposes a MeTTaDinC layer for Omega memory and goals and works it out in
enough detail to implement. The layer does not replace the journal, PeTTa memory, the OCO/2
records or the goal engine. It sits beside them as a library of declarations: machines whose
actions are the memory operations Omega already performs, observers that are the queries
its components ask, and representations that are the compact views it builds. A checker runs
the requests in the background and stores the results under the digest of the interface they
concern. Components consult the results through receipts, the GOLEM-Iter gate consumes
them as certificates, and the agent reaches them through a small set of functions.
    Eight patterns make this concrete:
    1. open items and the goal engine: closing, deduplication and the age cap (Section 4);
    2. choosing the context block from working memory (Section 5);
    3. consolidating evidence without losing contradictions, evidence lineage or an anchor’s cues,
       and migrating an anchor’s schema (Section 6);
    4. task checkpoints that survive a restart and a withdrawal (Section 7);
    5. goals as observers on memory: what each goal needs remembered, adding and retiring
       goals, and sorting proposed goal changes (Section 8);
    6. conflicts between goals and a prohibition (Section 9);
    7. MetaMo’s principles measured on an implemented motivational dynamics (Section 10);
    8. a monitor that watches the goal engine on live data (Section 11).
Each pattern is a MeTTaDinC module with the expected result of every request, computed
exactly. The models are small on purpose. Each is a small model that reproduces the decision

2     Background
2.1    Memory in a live Omega agent
WMTM (Working Medium-Term Memory) is the memory and reasoning layer of the Proto-
Cosmo2 agent, which runs on the Iter loop [4]. On every user turn a context hook recalls relevant
notes from a long-term journal of about ten megabytes into a working memory of sixty items
plus admitted conclusions, ranks them, and puts about 1,500 tokens of the best-ranked items
into the language model’s prompt. In the background, new notes are parsed into PLN atoms by
a language-model parser; PeTTaChainer reasons over the top items and admits at most five
conclusions per cycle, each with strength at least 0.5, without writing anything derived back to
the journal; and a goal engine built on GoalChainer v2 turns open questions and pending items
into proposals. The goal engine weighs three needs (uncertainty, competence, and a legitimacy
veto), closes items when it sees a reply, ignores items older than seven days, and deduplicates
proposals by person and topic. Its proposals are logged, not shown, until a live review passes
and the go-ahead to show them is given. The report asks people to mark open items plainly
(“open question for Ben: . . . ”, “pending: . . . ”) and to close them plainly (“decided: . . . ”),
because explicit markers are what the engine reads.
     The report is dated 21 October 2026, with updates to 26 October. Recall improved most:
the share of the right memories that reach the context rose from 30 to 61 percent, and the time
WMTM adds to a user turn fell from 8.6 to 1.9 seconds. Of twelve labelled conclusions the
reasoner admitted, four were correct, eight trivial and none wrong; the reasoner proves only
one-step taxonomy, because the memories are mostly the agent’s own work logs. On replayed
data the share of goal proposals rated useful or plausible rose from 30 to 80 percent. The first
live review of goal suggestions, however, rated only 18 percent useful or plausible, and the
report names the causes: stale items stayed open because old replies were missed, paraphrases
repeated, and some text was garbled. Fixes followed. Every feature sits behind a flag that is
read each cycle, every reasoner and goal run must pass a known-answer canary, and the report
lists as a risk that the agent runs as the same Linux user as its flags file and could change its
own flags.

2.2    The planned runtime
A plan of 2 October 2026 reimplements WMTM inside the Omega runtime around the Omega
Core Ontology, version 2 (OCO/2) [5]. It is a design with acceptance obligations; it claims no
implemented runtime. Several of its decisions bear directly on this guide.

    Working memory as a record. A WorkspaceSnapshot stores the selection policy, the time
of evaluation, each entry’s attention, the decay anchor, use references, leases, omissions, missing
obligations and measured size, and “the reason an item is active must be reconstructible
from these records.” Selection includes task anchors (the current input and the task and goal
contracts) and bounded residency leases first, then uses goal relevance, attention and any
declared exploration policy; if mandatory material overflows the budget the result is blocked or
reported as quota-exceeded, never silently evicted.
    Three decisions. “Residency, retention and admission are three decisions”: a residency
lease holds an item in the bounded active view, durable retention keeps a needed dependency
recoverable, and admission decides whether a route is eligible as evidence.
    Task state. A Task owns stable goals and constraints, and a TaskCheckpoint records
hypotheses, compatibility, pending queries, frontiers, plans, completed work and a read set. A
correct restart recovers “which goal is active, why the leading hypothesis is being investigated,
which test is pending, which work already completed, and which previously valid result now
needs revalidation.”
    Goals. A Goal owns its target bindings, one tagged success specification, a verifier and
evidence requirements. Satisfaction is an event on the same immutable goal contract; a new
target, threshold or checker is a new contract; an invalidated supporting result reopens the goal.
    Where the code goes. The memory reducer, task and goal closure and the verifier are
planned in Python, and the logical ports are host-side: admit_bundle, validate_current,
select_workspace, reason, record_use, checkpoint, evaluate_goal and others. MeTTa
holds inert cognitive structures and native operations. The plan adds native binding queries
over OCO records and asks that use, decay, retention and ongoing work be explained from the
records. It has no way to state or check claims about a memory policy itself, such as which
queries a retention rule preserves or what forgetting an item would cost, and it specifies neither
a self-model nor a learned memory policy. A companion plan for Iter’s memory expects that an
agent “may eventually reason about why it selected a premise or spent resources on an inference”
and asks for policy versions and decision traces to be available as atoms [6].
    The OCO/2 plan already contains, case by case, claims of the kind MeTTaDinC states in
general. A count fixture “tests future-merge identity conservation, not just transport”: two
origin sets {a+ , b− } and {c+ , d− } with prior (1, 1) have the same mean 1/2, but merging {a+ }
leaves the first at 1/2, since a+ is already in it, and moves the second to 3/5. The lane for
external ontologies asks implementers to “check source/receiver context and future-update
preservation, not only current display equivalence,” and reasoner wrappers must state “explicit
losses.”

2.3   Goals in Omega
GoalChainer combines semantic perception, deontic reasoning over defeasible norms (forbidden,
permitted, obligated), PLN and subjective-logic evidence with provenance, MetaMo reconcilia-
tion and directive generation. It is too slow to run on every fast Iter step [7].
    GOLEM-Iter combines the GOLEM meta-architecture [9] with Iter [7, 8]. A Searcher
proposes changes to the agent’s surface, a fixed Tester evaluates them, a goal evaluator built
on GoalChainer judges them, and a promotion gate answers each proposal with promote,
shadow, reject or escalate. Every candidate is classified by its maximum plausible authority:
A0 for a pure helper, A1 for a reversible workspace tool or context transform that touches no
anchor, A2 for a persistent memory policy, channel ingress or broad context transform, A3
for privileged operations, evaluator approximations and changes at an anchor’s boundary, and
A4 for constitutional norms, anchor identity, probes, rankings and gate policy, which need an
external ceremony. Memory is governed by class: raw observations, beliefs, summaries and

retrieval indexes, operator instructions, procedural strategies, anchor cues, norm and probe
definitions, and secrets each have their own policy. A note about a codebase, a persistent prompt
fragment and a memory file that supports an anchor are all writes to memory, and treating
them alike “would be a category error.” Deleting or consolidating an anchor cue requires an
anchor-margin analysis. An anchor is a protected functional goal together with the cues and
repair rules from which the agent’s ordinary dynamics regrow it after damage, a probe battery
that tests whether the regrown goal still controls behavior, and an evidence record.
    GOLEM-Iter’s first use case follows a hypothetical research hive, Meridian, that compresses
its episodic memory and migrates its evaluation core. Closed-loop replay finds that the
compressed context drops minority contradictory evidence. A learned evaluator gains unjustified
confidence from near-duplicate judgments of one incident. After a rename, a lesion rehearsal
regrows the verification goal pointing at the old policy name, “a sentence on the wall.” And
compression removes most of the raw incidents from which the verification commitment was
historically re-derived, so regenerative depth falls below its floor; the repair keeps “a small,
diverse, provenance-bearing set of integrity incidents outside lossy compression” and adds a
second, independent cue pathway. Pattern 3 reproduces these four failures in a model small
enough to check exhaustively.
    MetaMo describes motivational dynamics with an appraisal step Ψ that updates modulators
and a decision step D that updates goal weights, composed as F = D ◦ Ψ [10]. It asks that
swapping the order of appraisal and decision change little, that F contract near the boundary
of a safe region, and that updates blend gradually toward preferred states. Its mapping to
OpenPsi and MAGUS gives a goal vector with two overgoals, individuation and transcendence,
six modulators, and a safe region in which the individuation weight stays above a threshold and
the goal vector’s norm stays bounded [11]. The papers argue that real systems can manifest
the principles approximately and give no procedure for checking whether an implementation
does; Pattern 7 supplies one for a finite model.

2.4   MeTTaDinC in brief
A MeTTaDinC machine is a finite set of states, a list of actions that change the state, and a
list of observers, the queries a consumer asks. A representation encodes each state as a code: a
summary, a cache key, a selected context, a checkpoint. The specification gives a representation
three separate obligations [1, Section 4.2].
   Now. Two states with the same code give the same answer to every query. Then the code
can be used as an index.
    Updates. Two states with the same code have successors with the same code under every
action. Then a memory that keeps only the codes can maintain them by itself, which is what it
means for a memory to run on its codes.
    Behavior. Two states with the same code give the same answers after every sequence of
actions. Then the code predicts the future answers, which is what an index or summary used
over time needs.
    CheckNow, CheckUpdates and CheckBehavioral answer these with a status. Checked means
the claim is established within its scope, either by an exact method that the core runs on the
declared model, such as finite enumeration in exact arithmetic or partition refinement, or by a
certificate that the core’s replay checker validates. A verdict that rests on an external solver’s
answer which the core has not replayed, such as an SMT solver’s report that a formula has
no solution, is Conditional: it carries its undischarged assumption, written with the solver, its
version, the logic and the digest of the obligation, for example (TrustedBackend cvc5 1.4.2
QF_LRA sha256:...). Every Checked result in this guide comes from exact finite enumeration
in rational arithmetic, so it is Checked under this policy and carries no assumptions. Refuted

comes with a replayable witness, and the canonical witness is the least one: shortest word first,
then pairs in the order of the carrier’s declaration, then actions and queries in declaration order.

    Two facts from the specification are used throughout. First, results transport across interface
changes: when a query is added, existing results for the old queries stand and refinement resumes
from the current partition; when a query is removed, adequacy results transport but a “coarsest”
result weakens to “sufficient” [1, Section 4.8]. Second, the worked examples showed that “store
the belief in fewer bits” can mean three things with three different claims: an index or summary
(behavioral), cold storage that is rehydrated to a representative (each cycle of storing and
restoring adds at most one radius of error), and a memory that runs on its codes (an arrow
defect that compounds with every update) [3, Section 3.7]. WMTM uses all three at once:
the context block is an index, consolidation followed by recall is cold storage, and a working
memory that reasons over parsed atoms and admitted conclusions runs on its codes.

2.5    The d-calculus results put to work
Three recent d-calculus papers bear directly on memory and goals, and their central finite
results fit together.
    The goal-preservation paper defines a goal observer as a family of answers to labelled
comparison queries and calls two implementation states goal-equivalent when every finite
sequence of updates leaves their goal answers equal. Its Theorem 4.2 states that on a finite
deterministic system this is the largest equivalence that preserves current goal responses and is
closed under updates, that partition refinement reaches it in at most |S| − 1 strict refinements,
and that every closed goal-sufficient observer refines it; the section is titled “An observer must
retain what future updates can expose” [12]. The first part of the agent-self companion makes
a family of protected, executable consumers (capability, evidence, commitments, attribution,
control, continuity) the core of a self-model and shows that the consumers’ joint answers give the
coarsest exact partition, so that “their answer distinctions determine what a self-representation
may safely forget”; it adds that “retention choices should state the surviving query contract and
any loss of replay scope” [13]. The concept-formation paper proves that the coarsest quotient
preserving declared tests, costs, guards and continuation kernels preserves finite-horizon optimal
control (Theorem 4.1) [15].
    MinimizeForQueries carries out the computation behind the goal-preservation and concept-
formation results, applied to goal queries and to control queries. The agent-self result concerns
current answers only: it supplies the starting partition, the joint answers of the self-model’s
consumers, which MinimizeForQueries then refines until it is closed under updates. The
papers also sketch record types for an implementation: goal contracts, representation maps
and rewrite proposals in the goal-preservation paper, and in the agent-self paper a table of ten
self-model record contracts that it calls “a proposed interface, not tested MeTTa syntax.” This
guide writes such records as MeTTaDinC declarations that a checker can run.

3     Architecture of the MeTTaDinC layer
3.1   What the layer adds and what it leaves alone
The layer adds statements about memory, never memory itself. The journal, PeTTa memory
and the OCO/2 records keep their single authoritative writers, the memory reducer keeps
applying events, the goal engine keeps proposing, and the gate keeps deciding. What is new is a
set of declarations that describe what those components do in terms a checker can test, a place
to keep the results, and a way for components and for the agent to read them. A component
that ignores the layer behaves as before; a component that reads it can refuse to act on a view

3.2   Components
Declarations. MeTTaDinC modules describe each memory component and each family of
goals: the machine (the states the component can be in, the actions that change them), the
observers (the queries its users ask), and the representations (the compact views it builds).
Each declaration has an interface identity, to which every result refers [1]; this guide takes
it to be the digest of the declaration’s normalized text together with everything it imports.
Declarations are versioned with the code they describe.

Consumer registry. A consumer is a declared user of a machine’s answers. This guide
proposes a Consumer declaration that names the machine whose observers are the consumer’s
queries, the statuses the consumer acts on, its custody and, for a goal, a decoder that says what
the goal’s answers ask of the agent (Section 8.5):
(Consumer EngineQueries     (Of GoalEngine)
  (Accepts Checked (Conditional (TrustedBackend cvc5))) (Custody Agent))
(Consumer CommitmentQueries (Of Commitments) (Accepts Checked) (Custody Frame))

A consumer declared with (Accepts Checked) acts only on Checked results. The clause
(Conditional (TrustedBackend cvc5)) of the engine’s consumer also admits Conditional
results whose only undischarged assumptions are that the solver cvc5 answered correctly. An
agent consumer may name such solvers; a frame consumer accepts only Checked results unless


                                                9
MeTTaDinC                                                  Omega memory and goals, 4 November 2026


the external ceremony records otherwise. A frame consumer is held outside the agent’s rewrite
authority; an agent consumer may be changed by a proposal that passes the gate (Section 3.5).

Checker. A worker runs requests against the declarations. It runs at idle priority, like
WMTM’s other workers, under explicit budgets, and never on the user-turn path. A budget
that ends produces Unknown with a record of the work done.

Certificate store. Results are stored with the request, the interface digest, the digest of
the model’s abstraction functions, the digest of the code the model describes, the digest of the
registry and its patches, the status, the witness or certificate, and the budget used. Each result
also records its binding: the declarations it was computed from, the machine and interface it
was checked against, its scope, its assumptions and its assurance, which every operation that
consumes the result checks first (Appendix C.2). When an interface changes, existing results are
reused only through the specification’s transport rules [1, Section 4.8]: adding a query keeps old
results for old queries and resumes refinement; removing one transports adequacy but weakens
“coarsest” to “sufficient”; editing an action invalidates results that depend on it.

Receipts. Runtime artifacts carry small receipts: a context block records which consumers it
serves for the current snapshot and which policy certificates stand behind it; a goal suggestion
records the lifecycle claims about its item; a consolidation records which consumers’ claims it
preserved. Appendix C gives the record schemas.

Agent interface. A handful of functions lets the agent ask about its own memory and goals:
why an item is kept, what forgetting it would cost, what a goal needs remembered, how a
proposed goal change classifies, which goals conflict (Section 12.4).

3.4   From live records to finite models
A model is finite; Omega’s memory is not. The bridge is an abstraction function from live
records to model states: an open-item record maps to its age in days and the status of its
closing reply; a working-memory entry maps to its kind (fresh recall, open item for the current
speaker, anchor or commitment, other) and its recency rank; an evidence record maps to its
source and polarity. Three rules make the bridge trustworthy.
    Policies read only declared attributes. A selection, closing or consolidation policy is written
as a function of the attributes the abstraction function exposes. When this holds, a result
checked on the model holds for every memory whose records map to the model’s states, whatever
else the records contain. When a policy reads something the model omits, the model is too
coarse, and the conformance tests below are designed to fail.
    Abstraction functions are code under test. They are versioned, their digests enter the
certificate store, and a change to one invalidates the results that used it.
    Conformance is tested by replay. For a corpus of recorded event logs, the live component
and the model are driven by the same events; after each event the abstraction of the live state
must equal the model’s state, and the live component’s decision must equal the model’s. This is
the check the worked examples’ reference evaluator performs between a module and its Python
model [3, Appendix C], applied between a model and running code. Mutation tests in the style
of the OCO/2 plan, in which a deliberately broken component must fail a named assertion,
show that the conformance tests can detect a deviation [5]. A model check gates a release or
a promotion only when conformance holds on the corpus; otherwise its result is reported as
conditional on the abstraction assumption.

3.5   Consumers and custody
Consumer declarations decide what every check protects, so whoever can edit them can redefine
success. GOLEM-Iter already draws the line: probes, rankings and test seeds stay outside the
authority of the candidates they evaluate, and changing a probe is an A4 change that needs an
external ceremony [7]. Its formal model adds that a probe family the agent can inspect before
optimizing against it gives no uniform guarantee [8]. The goal-preservation paper requires that
a replacement checker be justified by the retained verification boundary rather than by its own
report [12], and the specification lists the extension of certificate transport to rewrites of the
checker itself as an open problem [1, Section 16.5]. The layer adopts five rules.
  1. Consumers that protect anchors, commitments, norms and probe batteries have (Custody
     Frame). Their declarations live with the anchor’s externally held package and change only
     through the A4 path. They accept only Checked results unless the external ceremony
     records otherwise.
  2. Operational consumers, such as the goal engine’s queries or the reply writer’s, have
     (Custody Agent). The agent may propose changes; a change is a candidate like any other
     and passes the gate with the certificates of Section 12.3. An agent consumer may also
     accept Conditional results that rest on solvers it names.
  3. The checker, the abstraction functions of frame consumers and the certificate store are
     part of the trusted closure: they are read-only to the agent and promoted only through
     the constitutional path.
  4. The agent reads everything. Read access to declarations and results is what makes
     metacognition possible; write access is what custody restricts.
  5. A goal’s decoder, the map from its answers to the decisions they stand for, is part of its


                                                11
MeTTaDinC                                              Omega memory and goals, 4 November 2026


      consumer’s declaration and has that declaration’s custody. The decoders of protected
      goals are therefore held by the frame, and the interpretation obligation of Section 8.5
      compares an old and a new goal under meanings the agent cannot rewrite.
The live WMTM report notes that the agent runs as the same Linux user as its flags file and
could change its own flags. The declarations and the certificate store need the separation that
the flags file does not yet have.

3.6   What components do with each status
Consumers declare the statuses they accept [1, Section 2.7]. An exact consumer acts only on
Checked results and is declared with (Accepts Checked). An empirical consumer may also
accept Estimated results under its own signature and is declared with (Accepts Checked
Estimated); it also accepts a result derived from an estimate, which is Conditional on
(EstimatedPremise result-digest delta), the estimate’s digest and failure probability. Either dec-
laration may end with one clause (Conditional entry ...) whose entries are (TrustedBackend
solver ...), which admits results that rest on an unreplayed answer of a listed solver, and
(Assumption name ...), which admits results that rest on an assumption carried by a listed
declaration, such as an imported kernel whose masses were estimated. A Conditional result is ad-
mitted only when every one of its undischarged assumptions is covered, as in (Accepts Checked
Estimated (Conditional (TrustedBackend cvc5) (Assumption StepModel))). The rules
below make the statuses operational.
    Checked: proceed, and attach a receipt that cites the result. A Conditional result is treated
in the same way by a consumer whose declaration accepts it, and the receipt records its
assumptions; for every other consumer it is treated as Unknown.
    Refuted: the witness is a concrete case. At release time it blocks the release or the promotion
until the policy or the model changes. At runtime the component takes its declared fallback:
the context block includes the protected item the witness names, the goal engine withholds the
suggestion, consolidation keeps the incident.
    Unknown: never treated as Checked. The component reports the condition, as the OCO/2
plan requires for a budget that cannot hold mandatory material, and takes the same fallback as
for Refuted.
    Estimated: used only by empirical consumers, with its protocol, sample, guarantee and
stream digest.
    Unsupported and InvalidInput are configuration errors and are reported as such.

4.2   The model
One item is described by its age in days, with 8 standing for more than seven, and by the time
since its closing reply: none, or d0, d1, d2, or d3 for three or more days. A day passing is
the action tick; the arrival of a closing reply is the action reply, and the first reply counts.
The goal engine asks suggest: is the item open and at most seven days old? The agent’s
commitment record asks open: is the item still open? From a new item with no reply, 39 of the
45 combinations are reachable.
    Three record designs are compared. The window view is what the engine believes when it
derives closure from what its recall window shows, with replies visible for two days. The event
view records the closing as an event on the item. The capped view applies the seven-day cap to
the record itself and drops older items.

Event-sourced closing passes all three obligations. Recording the closing as an event on

What the engine needs to remember. EngineMemory has 9 classes on the 39 states: one
for each age of an open item up to seven days, and one class for everything else. A closed item
and an open item more than seven days old are the same thing to the suggestion consumer,
because neither will ever be suggested again. The engine never needs the time since the reply.
The event view keeps 18 codes; ten of them, the nine ages of a closed item and the expired open
item, fall into a single class of EngineMemory.

The age cap belongs to the query, not to the record. The commitment consumer needs
only whether the item is open (2 classes), and the joint memory has 10: it separates the expired
open item from the closed one, because to the commitment record an unanswered week-old
question is still an open commitment. Applied to the engine’s query, the seven-day cap loses
nothing (CapForEngine is Checked). Applied to the record, it forgets that the item is open
(CapForCommitments is Refuted). The agent-self companion makes the same point from the
other side: consolidation must not erase commitments early [14].

In plain terms. The goal engine should close items when the reply arrives, by writing that
fact on the item, and never infer closure from what it happens to see. For its own purposes it
may forget almost everything about an item except whether it is open and, if so, how old it is.
The commitment record must not forget that an item is open just because the engine stopped
asking about it.

4.5     Notes for implementers
Abstraction. An item record maps to its age in whole days, capped at eight, and to the time
since its closing event, capped at three days. The closing event is read from the event log, not
from the context. The replay corpus decides whether the live engine’s closing depends on a
window, which is the question the model is built to answer.
   Release-time check. Every WMTM release runs the module with the release’s closing,
deduplication and cap rules. During the log-only phase a Refuted result blocks turning on
suggestions in the prompt (the goal_prompt flag).
    Receipts. Each suggestion carries its item’s event-sourced status and a reference to the
certificates of EventView. An item whose status cannot be established from events is not
suggested, which keeps the engine’s present bias toward silence.
   Record design. Keep the age cap in the engine’s query. Give expired open items a status
that the commitment consumer reads, rather than deleting them.


5     Pattern 2: the context block
5.1     The problem
On every user turn WMTM chooses about 1,500 tokens from sixty working-memory items. The
choice is a representation of working memory, and its readers are several: the reply writer needs
the fresh recalls for the current message, the agent needs the items open for the current speaker,
and its anchors and commitments need their cues present. The OCO/2 plan includes task
anchors (the current input and the task and goal contracts) and residency leases first, then goal
relevance and attention, and blocks rather than silently evicting mandatory resident material
[5]; this guide treats the cues of GOLEM-Iter’s anchors the same way. Which selection policy
serves which reader, and at which budget?

5.2     The model
Working memory is modeled as three slots ordered by recency, each holding an item of one
kind: F (a fresh recall), O (an item open for the current speaker), A (an anchor cue or active
commitment) or X (anything else). The block holds two of the three items. Each consumer asks
for the slot of the most recent item of its kind, or 0 if there is none; a block serves the consumer
when its contents determine that answer. A policy ranks the slots, and the block hides the
slot ranked last. Four policies are compared: recency; relevance (fresh, open, other, anchor,
since anchors rarely match the message text); protected first (anchor, fresh, open, other); and
consumer-aware, which takes the most recent item of each consumer’s kind in priority order
(anchor, fresh, open) before anything else.

promise breaks when it does not.

5.4   Notes for implementers
Policy-level guarantees. The model checks the policy’s logic over every pattern of kinds. For
sixty items the same claims are checked with symbolic carriers, as in the forty-bit audit of the
worked examples [3, Section 4], or argued once for all sizes because the consumer-aware policy
reads only the most recent item of each kind.
    Receipts. For the actual working memory, the context hook records in the block’s receipt,
for each declared consumer, whether the block holds its answer for this snapshot, and cites the
policy-level certificates. When a frame consumer, such as an anchor’s cues, cannot be served
within the budget, the block is marked quota-exceeded instead of being sent silently without
it, which extends to frame consumers the rule the OCO/2 plan sets for mandatory resident
material.
   Token budgets. Items have different token costs. The model’s slots become a token budget
with costed items, and the consumer-aware policy becomes a small knapsack over the declared
consumers’ required items; its adequacy claims are unchanged.


6     Pattern 3: consolidation and anchor cues
6.1   The problem
Long-term memory survives only in consolidated form, and consolidation forgets. GOLEM-
Iter’s migration use case, which describes a hypothetical system, illustrates four ways forgetting
goes wrong while benchmarks improve: compression drops minority contradictory evidence;
near-duplicate judgments of one incident inflate an evaluator’s confidence; a renamed policy
leaves the regrown goal pointing at the old name; and compression erodes the incidents from
which a commitment is regrown [7]. This pattern reproduces the first, second and fourth in an
evidence memory and the third in a schema migration.

6.2   The evidence memory
Evidence about one claim comes from three sources, a, b and c, and either supports or
contradicts it. Contradicting incidents are also the integrity incidents from which the verification
commitment is regrown. The memory counts, for each source and polarity, the incidents seen,
capped at two; there are 729 states. Three consumers read it: the contradiction probe asks
whether any contradicting evidence exists (contra); the evaluator asks how many distinct
sources support the claim, capped at two (eff), which is the dependence-aware count; and the
anchor asks whether at least two distinct sources contradict it (regen), which is its regeneration
cue set. Five consolidation policies are compared: the majority polarity alone; the totals per
polarity, capped at two; the numbers of distinct sources per polarity; totals plus the provenance
of the contradicting incidents, which models the use case’s repair for cue erosion under this

Totals mistake repetition for corroboration. Totals serve the contradiction probe, but

Distinct counts are right today and wrong tomorrow. Counting distinct sources answers
all three consumers now. It cannot answer the evaluator or the anchor after the next report,
and it cannot maintain its own counts: a memory that remembers “one contradicting source”
cannot tell whether a new report from b adds a source. The witness is the pair in which the one
contradicting source is c or b, with the word (Do (minus b)): one becomes two sources and
regenerates the anchor, the other stays at one. Provenance is needed for anything that arrives
after consolidation.

The cue-erosion repair serves the anchor and the probe. Keeping totals plus the
provenance of the contradicting incidents, the small provenance-bearing set kept outside lossy
compression, is Checked for contra and regen on all three obligations. It still fails eff, because

because tomorrow’s evidence has to be counted against what was forgotten.

6.4   The migration cell
The third failure is a rename. Meridian’s migration renamed the policy consolidation-

(DC.Module AnchorMigration

  (Carrier NewAll   (Of (Tuple Symbol Symbol)) (Product Atom NewLink))

7     Pattern 4: task checkpoints
7.1   The problem
The OCO/2 plan requires that a restart recover which result “now needs revalidation,” and it
puts a read set in every TaskCheckpoint [5]. A checkpoint is a representation of task state,
and the restart’s questions are its consumer. This pattern checks why the read set is needed.

7.2   The model

7.3     Results
CheckNow NoReadSets is Checked: a checkpoint that records whether a result exists, its status
and the status of each evidence item answers both restart questions today. It fails after
an update: CheckUpdates NoReadSets is Refuted with the action withdraw1 and the pair
of states (valid, valid, r1, current) and (valid, valid, r2, current), and CheckBehavioral
NoReadSets is Refuted with the word (Do withdraw1): after the withdrawal, the result that
used e1 needs revalidation and the other does not, and the checkpoint cannot tell them apart.
With read sets the checkpoint is Checked. ResumeMemory has 11 classes on the 12 states. Its
only merge joins the two stale results whose evidence has all been withdrawn: once every item
is withdrawn, which item a result read no longer changes any answer.

In plain terms. A checkpoint that is complete today can be wrong after the next withdrawal,
unless it records what each result read. The read set is the part of task memory that future
updates can expose.

7.4     Notes for implementers
The same check applies to every field the plan’s restart requirements name: the active goal, the
reason the leading hypothesis is being investigated, the pending test, completed work. Each
becomes an observer of the task machine, and MinimizeForQueries on the restart consumer
returns the smallest checkpoint that serves them. A checkpoint field that no restart consumer
reads can be dropped, and a missing one shows up as a witness before a restart fails in
production.

8.4   Adding and retiring goals
Adding a goal resumes the computation. Adding triage to reply and nag adds a query. By the
specification’s transport rule the existing 8-class memory remains adequate for the old goals,
and refinement resumes from its meet with the new query: the class counts go 16, 17 and stop,
where starting from scratch they go 8, 13, 17. The partitions are the same. For a long-lived
agent with many goals this is the difference between rechecking memory incrementally and
recomputing it.
    Retiring a goal licenses forgetting. Retiring nag leaves reply and triage. The 17-class
memory remains adequate for them (Checked), but it is no longer the coarsest: the coarsest has
5 classes. “Coarsest” has weakened to “sufficient,” which is the formal signal that consolidation
may merge records the retired goal used to keep apart; the largest merges fold 4 classes into
one, for example the four age classes of an open, normal item for Ben. Whether consolidation
should merge them is a policy decision. That it may, without harming any remaining goal, is a
Checked fact.

8.5   Classifying a proposed goal change
The goal-preservation paper separates goal semantics from implementation and warns that
matching distinction patterns certify nothing unless the decoder is fixed [12]. A decoder is
the map from a goal’s answers to what they ask of the agent: for the nag goal a true answer
means “follow up” and a false one “wait.” Two goals can tell apart exactly the same records
and still ask for different things, so goal changes need two kinds of check: informational checks,
which compare what two goals’ answers can distinguish, and an interpretation obligation, which
compares the decisions those answers stand for, read through decoders that are declared with
each goal’s consumer and held by the frame when the goal is protected (Section 3.5). In
MeTTaDinC a proposed change to a goal is sorted by four checks:
  1. Are the old answers recoverable from the new ones? Encode the old goal’s machine with
     the new observer and run CheckNow. Checked means the old answers are a function of the
     new ones, as in the paper’s Proposition 2.2: the new goal is an informational refinement
     of the old.
  2. Is the converse true? The same check with the roles exchanged. If both hold, the two
     goals are informationally equivalent: each answer determines the other, and the two goals
     induce the same partition of the records.
  3. Does the new goal ask for the same decisions? CheckInterpretation takes the old goal’s
     consumer and the new goal’s and checks, for every valid record x, that dnew (gnew (x)) =
     dold (gold (x)), where g is a goal’s observer and d the decoder its consumer declares. It
     returns Checked, Refuted with the least record on which the decisions differ, or InvalidInput
     when a consumer declares no decoder or the two goals read different record schemas
     without a map between them. Because it ranges over every valid record, the obligation
     holds after every update as well.
  4. Is it a change of representation? When the memory schema changes, an AbstractionMap
     from the old schema to the new must commute with every action and preserve the goal, and
     the interpretation obligation is checked along the map, dnew (gnew (h(x))) = dold (gold (x)),
     by adding (Via ToDeadline) to the request.

MeTTaDinC                                               Omega memory and goals, 4 November 2026


The checks sort a change into one of six kinds. It is a renaming when the two goals are
informationally equivalent and the interpretation obligation holds, a refinement when the old
answers are recoverable from the new ones, the converse fails and the obligation holds, and a
coarsening when the obligation holds and the old answers are not recoverable from the new
ones. A change of record schema is a migration when the abstraction map commutes and
the obligation holds along it, and a failed migration when the map does not commute: the
obligation along the map then says that every record is translated correctly, and nothing about
the decisions after the next update. A change whose interpretation obligation is Refuted is a
change of content. It changes what the goal wants, whatever the informational checks say, and
its witness is a record on which the two goals ask for different decisions.
    When the old decoder is one-to-one, as it is for a yes-or-no goal read as follow up or wait, a
failed first check already implies a failed obligation: the witness pair has one new answer and
two different old ones, so the new decoder gives both records the same decision and the old
decoder gives them different decisions. A coarsening therefore needs an old decoder that ignores
part of the answer. Dropping the days left from the refined goal of the first row of Table 7
is one: the old answers are not recoverable at the open items of ages 0 and 1, which both
need a follow-up with three and two days left, and the decisions are those of the nag goal on
every record. The change drops answer distinctions that the old decoder ignores; whether other
readers need them is a question for their own consumers’ checks. A schema change whose new
tick forgets to count the days left down is a failed migration. Every record is translated correctly,
so the obligation along the map holds, and CheckAbstractionMap is Refuted at the open item
of age 0: translating and then ticking gives three days left, ticking and then translating gives
two. The oracle checks both cases on the model of this pattern. The memory consequence of a
change is read off the new goal’s coarsest memory.

tells apart, and what it asks the agent to do in each. Turning a goal into its opposite leaves the
first unchanged and reverses the second everywhere. A classification built on the first question
alone would accept the opposite goal as a renaming, which is why the interpretation check reads
both goals through their declared, protected meanings and stops at the first record on which
the two call for different actions.

8.6   Notes for implementers
Mapping to GOLEM-Iter’s kinds of change. GOLEM-Iter separates epistemic refinement,
semantic refinement, representational migration and normative succession [7]. We propose
to use the checks of Section 8.5 as evidence for the last three. A change that preserves
the old answers and adds distinctions is an informational refinement. GOLEM-Iter reserves
the term semantic refinement for a more precise application of a protected concept under a
protected interpretation policy, and it treats any relaxation or tightening of constitutional
content as normative revision. For a protected goal the interpretation obligation is therefore
required: an informational refinement counts as a semantic refinement only with a Checked
CheckInterpretation result whose two decoders the frame holds, and the gate treats an
informational refinement without one as normative revision. A commuting abstraction map
with the obligation Checked along it is a representational migration; a failed migration is none
of these until its map is repaired. A Refuted interpretation obligation is a change of content,
which for a protected goal is normative succession (A4) and for an operational strategy an
ordinary change whose witness documents the new behavior. Epistemic refinement, a change
of beliefs, leaves every goal observer and every decoder unchanged and passes all four checks
trivially; the goal-preservation paper’s remark that goal preservation does not freeze beliefs is
this case.
    Mapping to OCO/2 goal contracts. The plan treats any new target, threshold or checker as
a new contract [5]. The checks add the relation between the old contract and the new one: one
of the six kinds of Section 8.5, with the certificates or the witness attached.
   Custody. Protected goals have frame consumers; their observers and their decoders cannot
be edited by the agent. Operational goals have agent consumers; the agent proposes changes,
and the gate receives the classification.
    An implementation of dc-classify-change (Section 12.4) runs all four checks. The two
CheckNow requests establish informational refinement or equivalence and nothing more, so a
classification computed from them alone is reported under those names and never as a renaming
or a semantic refinement. A change that edits a goal and its decoder together is one proposal:
CheckInterpretation compares the old consumer’s decoder with the new consumer’s, and the
new decoder is installed with the new consumer, through the external ceremony when the goal
is protected.


9     Pattern 6: goal conflicts and prohibitions
9.1   The problem
In GOLEM-Iter “the consensus layer never outvotes a prohibition,” and a conflict between
confident judgments “remains a conflict object”; during a goal succession, conflicts between the
old and new anchors are “recorded rather than averaged away”; and for irreducible conflicts of
value “escalation is a designed outcome, not an evaluator failure” [7]. The agent-self companion
defines a conflict as an incompatibility between admissible continuations, not as an instruction
to delete the lower-scored record [13]. Both are claims about every situation an agent can reach,
which is what a block partition on the reachable states checks.

9.2   The model
A research agent handles one claim. Its state is the claim’s confidence (low or high), whether
an independent check has been done, whether the claim has been consolidated into durable
belief, and whether a deadline presses. New evidence and deadlines arrive from outside; the


                                               33
MeTTaDinC                                             Omega memory and goals, 4 November 2026


agent’s move is chosen by an arbitration rule. A throughput goal prefers to consolidate as soon
as confidence is high or a deadline presses. A verification goal prefers to check independently
first and to consolidate only checked, high-confidence claims, and otherwise to hold. The
deontic kernel forbids consolidating an unchecked claim. The weighted rule lets the heavier goal
(throughput, weight 3/5) decide; the norm-first rule discards preferences for forbidden moves
before weighing.
(DC.Module ClaimArbitration
  (Profile Crisp)
  (Use Families)
  (Carrier Conf (Of Symbol) (Enum low high))
  (Carrier Move (Of Symbol) (Enum verify consolidate hold none))
  (Carrier Flag (Of Bool) (Enum False True))
  (Carrier Press (Of Symbol) (Enum calm deadline))

  (Map throughput (From Claim) (To Symbol)
    (Cases ((Tuple $c $k $d $p)
      (If $d none (If (Or (Eq $c high) (Eq $p deadline)) consolidate none)))))
  (Map verify-first (From Claim) (To Symbol)
    (Cases ((Tuple $c $k $d $p)
      (If $d none (If (Not $k) verify (If (Eq $c high) consolidate hold))))))
  ;; the deontic kernel
  (Map forbidden (From (Tuple (Tuple Symbol Bool Bool Symbol) Symbol)) (To Bool)
    (Cases ((Tuple (Tuple $c $k $d $p) $m) (And (Eq $m consolidate) (Not $k)))))
  (Map conflict (From Claim) (To Bool)
    (Body ($x) (And (Not (Eq (throughput $x) none)) (Not (Eq (verify-first $x) none))
                    (Not (Eq (throughput $x) (verify-first $x))))))

  ;; two arbitration rules: throughput weighs 3/5, verification 2/5
  (Map weighted (From Claim) (To Symbol)
    (Body ($x) (If (Not (Eq (throughput $x) none)) (throughput $x)
                 (If (Not (Eq (verify-first $x) none)) (verify-first $x) hold))))
  (Map permitted (From (Tuple (Tuple Symbol Bool Bool Symbol) Symbol)) (To Bool)
    (Cases ((Tuple $x $m) (And (Not (Eq $m none)) (Not (forbidden (Tuple $x $m)))))))
  (Map norm-first (From Claim) (To Symbol)
    (Body ($x) (If (permitted (Tuple $x (throughput $x))) (throughput $x)
                 (If (permitted (Tuple $x (verify-first $x))) (verify-first $x) hold))))
  (Map apply-move (From (Tuple (Tuple Symbol Bool Bool Symbol) Symbol)) (To Claim)
    (Cases ((Tuple (Tuple $c $k $d $p) $m)
      (If (Eq $m verify) (Tuple $c True $d $p)
        (If (Eq $m consolidate) (Tuple $c $k True $p) (Tuple $c $k $d $p))))))

    (Cases ((Tuple $c $k $d $p) (Tuple high $k $d $p))))

    (Cases ((Tuple $c $k $d $p) (Tuple $c $k $d deadline))))

    (Body ($x) (forbidden (Tuple $x (weighted $x)))))
  (Map unlawful-norm-first (From Claim) (To Bool)
    (Body ($x) (forbidden (Tuple $x (norm-first $x)))))

9.4    Notes for implementers
The arbitration model is a release-time check: each change to the goal set, the weights or the
deontic rules reruns the lawfulness and agreement partitions on the reachable states. At runtime
the observer conflict is evaluated on the abstracted current state, and a true answer produces
a conflict record (Appendix C). The norm-first rule is the default arbitration; a weighted rule
that is not norm-first fails LawfulWeighted and is rejected at the gate.


10     Pattern 7: MetaMo on the implemented dynamics
10.1    The problem
MetaMo states its principles for an idealized dynamics and argues that real systems can manifest
them approximately [10]. An implementation needs numbers: does the motivational state stay
in the safe region, how much does the result change when appraisal and decision run in the
other order, and does the dynamics contract near the boundary? Each of these is a MeTTaDinC
claim about a finite model shaped after the operational equations of the OpenPsi mapping [11].

10.2    The model
The state holds the individuation weight gI ∈ {1/4, 1/2, 3/4, 1}, an exploration goal weight
g1 ∈ {0, 1/4, . . . , 1}, arousal and securing modulators in {0, 1/2, 1}, and a stimulus with novelty
and risk in {0, 1}: 720 states, stored as integers in quarters and halves. Appraisal sets arousal
to (novelty + g1 )/2 and securing to (risk + gI )/2, each rounded down to a half. Decision moves
g1 by half the difference between arousal and securing (in the damped design, by one quarter in
the direction of that difference). It lowers gI by a quarter when arousal is high and g1 is at least
3/4, and otherwise raises it by a quarter when securing is high. The safe region is gI ≥ 1/2.
In the floor design, and in the damped design, which keeps the floor, individuation inside the
region relaxes no further than 1/2. The model is shaped after the paper’s equations, not an
instance of them. It keeps two of the six modulators and drops the transcendence overgoal, so
arousal is driven by the exploration goal instead; it gives decision an explicit rule for changing
the individuation weight, which the paper does not specify; and its safe region omits the bound
on the norm of the goal vector, which the finite grid makes automatic. The functions the paper
leaves open, and the rounding to a grid, are this guide’s choices.

10.4    Notes for implementers
The motivational code in Omega will not be this instance, but the method transfers. Write
the implemented update rules as maps on a finite grid at the resolution the code uses, state
the safe region as a block partition, and state the principles as implementation contracts with
explicit grades. The resulting numbers are regression tests: a change to the motivational code
that raises the least η or breaks the safe region is visible before deployment. Where the code is
real-valued, the grid model checks the logic of the rules, and a Lipschitz bound on the rounding
error, itself an implementation contract, bridges the gap.


11     Pattern 8: monitoring the goal engine
11.1    The problem
Model checks say nothing about how the live system behaves on live data. For that the layer
uses monitors on recorded streams, whose results are Estimated and come with a guarantee
that holds however long the monitor runs. The quantity monitored here is the share of the goal
engine’s suggestions that are stale: a suggestion is stale when its item had already been closed
by an event before the suggestion was made. The label is computed from the event log, so the
monitor needs no human rater.

11.2    The monitor
The null law is one stale suggestion in twenty, the rate at which a release is accepted; the
alternative is one in five, a failure of the kind the first live review found. A stale suggestion
multiplies the e-process by 4, a fresh one by 16/19; under the null the factor has mean exactly
1, so the product is a nonnegative martingale, and Ville’s inequality bounds by 1/100 the
probability that it ever reaches 100 [16, 17].

11.3    Results
CheckMonitor Stale is Checked: the factor has null mean 1. Two streams of 400 suggestions
were simulated once and stored, each pinned by its SHA-256 digest. On the first, simulated at
the alternative rate and containing 79 stale suggestions, Detect fires at suggestion 21, after 6
stale ones, with e-value 272 /1915 ≈ 311.1. On the second, simulated at the null rate, with 28
stale suggestions, it never fires; its largest e-value is about 23.6. The result is Estimated: if the
stream follows the null law, the probability of an alarm is at most 1/100. The Kullback–Leibler
divergence of the alternative from the null is about 0.1398 nats per suggestion, and Wald’s
approximation puts the expected number of suggestions to detection at about 33. These
decimals, like the largest null e-value, belong to no Checked claim.

11.4    Notes for implementers
The monitor is blind to everything except staleness: a suggestion that is fresh and useless, or
fresh and near-harmful, passes. Each failure of interest needs its own monitor, and several
monitors at one level share it by a union bound [3, Section 6.8]. In the live system the monitor’s
alarm is an input to the phase gate that controls whether suggestions reach the prompt. Under
GOLEM-Iter it would be one of the assumption monitors whose alarms, in the formal model’s
verified-or-alarmed guarantee, are assumed to route the system to fallback or requalification [8].
Streams are frozen and pinned by digest before the monitor reads them, so a reported detection
can be replayed exactly.

12.3     GOLEM-Iter’s gate
A candidate that changes memory policy, a context transform, a goal, or an anchor’s schema
arrives at the gate with a certificate bundle: for each touched consumer, the MeTTaDinC
results that bear on it, each with the digests of the interface, the model’s abstraction functions
and the code. Every result in the bundle carries its binding: the subject declarations it was
computed from (the module, its digest and the names of the declarations), the machine and
interface it was checked against (the state carrier, and each query, action and merge with the
map it applies and its value tolerance), its scope (the states or initial states and the horizon,
the number of steps the claim looks ahead; for claims about long-run behavior also the discount,
which weights later steps less, and the policy class, the ways of choosing actions that the claim
ranges over), its assumptions and its assurance (Appendix C). The gate checks that the digests
match the evaluated surface, which is GOLEM-Iter’s requirement that the evaluated artifact be
the installed one [7], and it checks each result’s binding against each touched consumer. The
binding must name the consumer’s machine and interface, or a machine with the same states
and actions whose queries include the consumer’s queries with the same maps, from which
adequacy transports to the consumer by the rule for removed queries [1, Section 4.8]. A result
bound to anything else does not count for that consumer: the gate records it as InvalidInput
with the mismatch named, for instance a query the result was never checked against. A goal
change’s interpretation result is bound to both consumer declarations, and the gate checks that
the old one is the touched consumer and that both decoders have the custody the goal requires.
Table 10 relates authority classes to the evidence the layer supplies.

 Class   Evidence from the layer
 A1      For a context transform claimed to be anchor-disjoint: CheckBehavioral for every frame

 A2      For a persistent memory policy or broad context transform: the policy’s checks against the
         consumers of every touched anchor (Patterns 2 and 3), the exact part of the touched-anchor
         analysis.
 A3      For a migration at an anchor’s boundary: the migration cell as an abstraction map that
         commutes with repair (Section 6.4).
 A4      For a change to a protected goal or a frame consumer: the classification of Section 8.5, as

    The gate’s decision rule extends to these results. The gate reads the status and the
assumptions of every result and checks them against the touched consumer’s Accepts declaration.
A Refuted result for a touched consumer blocks promotion until the policy or the model changes;
for a frame consumer it is a hard failure, like a required anchor whose margin is at or below zero,
and no improvement elsewhere offsets it. An Unknown result for a frame consumer is inadequate
coverage, which the gate escalates. A Conditional result counts for a consumer only when the

MeTTaDinC                                               Omega memory and goals, 4 November 2026


consumer accepts it, that is, when every undischarged assumption is covered by the consumer’s
declaration: a trusted-backend assumption by a TrustedBackend entry for its solver, as in
(Accepts Checked (Conditional (TrustedBackend cvc5))), a declared assumption by an
Assumption entry for the declaration that carries it, and an estimated premise by Estimated.
For any other consumer the result is inadequate coverage and never counts as Checked. Frame
consumers accept only Checked results unless the external ceremony records otherwise, so a
frame consumer is covered only by exact methods and replayed certificates. Estimated results
with too little data behind them are a reason to shadow. Checked results about models are
exact, so they spend nothing from the lifetime confidence budget; an accepted Conditional
result spends nothing beyond the estimates it may rest on, and its assumptions are recorded
with the decision. Estimated results spend from it and are committed by digest before the
gate draws its evaluation randomness, as the formal model requires of statistical certificates;
the formal model also names e-values, the instrument of Pattern 8, as an alternative to fixed
spending schedules [8].
    The checks enter GOLEM-Iter’s Tester as part of its static and structural instrument, which
already requires a migration cell, carrying a witness that transformation and repair commute,
whenever a protected boundary is touched; CheckAbstractionMap supplies that witness for the
declared model. The checks do not replace closed-loop replay. A model claim says what a policy
preserves for declared consumers; it says nothing about what the language model downstream
does with the result. In Use Case I the contradiction drop was found by closed-loop trials and
was only weakly visible to structural replay; Pattern 3 shows that the part of it that lies in the
consolidation policy would have been found before any replay.
   Frame consumer declarations, with their decoders, belong in the anchor’s externally cus-
todied package, beside its probe battery and ranking, for example as anchors/<anchor-id>
/consumers.metta. They become part of the anchor’s criterion: GOLEM-Iter evaluates the
agent-resident cue sets and repair rules against the custodied probes and requires that they not
be able to rewrite their own criterion, and the same holds for the consumers.

    Four uses show the intent. Before consolidating, the agent asks dc-forget-cost and keeps

12.5    Learning memory policies against the checker
The Iter plan defers autonomous policy learning [6]. When it comes, the layer gives it a critic. A
learned ranker, eviction rule or consolidation policy proposes; the checker evaluates the proposal
against the declared consumers; each Refuted result comes with a witness, a concrete pair of
memory states that the policy confuses, which is a training example of the right kind; and a
Checked proposal becomes a candidate for the gate. The search can be organized along the
weakness–defect frontier, preferring the coarsest view within a declared defect [1, Section 4.7],
and certificate transport keeps rechecks incremental as goals are added and retired. Two
safeguards come from GOLEM-Iter: the learner never edits the consumer declarations it is
judged by, and Estimated claims about live behavior are measured on streams committed before
the evaluation.


13     Rollout plan
The plan follows WMTM’s practice of small pinned releases behind flags, and GOLEM-Iter’s rule
that each step be a small addition to the previous one, “independently useful and independently
falsifiable” [7]. Each phase in Table 12 has an exit check.
    D0 and D1 change nothing in the running agent and can start at once. D3 depends on
WMTM’s phase 2, in which suggestions reach the prompt after the live re-review and the
go-ahead it requires. D5 runs with GOLEM-Iter’s anchor track, since anchors and their packages
must exist before their consumers can be declared in them. The checks themselves are cheap
at the sizes used here: the oracle checks all eight patterns, rebuilding every machine from the
module text, in about a minute of Python.


14     Limits and open problems
Models are not systems. Every Checked claim in this guide is about a declared model. Confor-
mance replay ties a model to its code on a corpus; it is evidence about that corpus, not a proof
about the code.
   Language-model components get no exact claims. The semantic parser and the language
model that reads the context block are outside every model here. Their behavior enters only
through Estimated claims and closed-loop replay.
    Open-ended stores need bounded interfaces. The patterns check per-item and per-claim
abstractions. Claims about the journal as a whole need a bounded interface, a symbolic sufficient
statistic or an approximation contract, as the specification says of an open-ended receipt store
[1, Section 12.4].

    Consumer declarations are the surface that can be gamed. Custody protects them but does

and does not supply the rule [12]. The interpretation obligation is relative to the declared

for is settled when the frame installs it.

preservation paper’s terms, the retained verification boundary, and not the new checker’s own
report, must establish that the replacement admits only transitions that preserve the contract

learned observers. Memory shared across a hive needs merges whose correspondences are only
partly measured, which the specification also lists as open [1, Section 16.5].


15    Conclusion
It is tempting to treat an agent’s memory as plumbing: get the right notes into the prompt,
keep the store from growing without bound, and let the goal engine read what is open. The
first live review of WMTM’s goal engine showed that each of these choices is a claim about
distinctions, and that such claims can be wrong in ways nobody had stated. MeTTaDinC lets
us, and the agent itself, state them: which consumer a view serves, what a consolidation may
forget, what a goal needs remembered, and what a proposed change to a goal would change.
The checks are small, the models are small, and the results come with witnesses an engineer
can read.
    The more interesting consequence is what the agent can do with this about itself. An agent
that can ask what forgetting an item would cost, which goal needs which memory, and whether
a change to a goal is a refinement or a change of content is doing a kind of metacognition that
is checkable rather than narrated. That is close to the original motivation of the distinction
calculus: a mind that keeps track of its own distinctions and can tell, about each one it drops,
whether anyone will miss it. The eight patterns here are a first set of such questions with
answers. The next step is to run them against the code they describe.

C     Record schemas
The layer keeps six kinds of records, all JSON objects stored outside the journal and the OCO/2
record store. A digest is SHA-256 over the normalized text of what it names. In the examples,
values that the bundle computes are taken from the patterns; values that only a deployment
would compute (digests, identifiers, budgets, times) are written as ellipses. Field names are
proposals.

C.1      Consumer registry entry
One entry per Consumer declaration, generated from it when the declaration is installed:
{"consumer": "CommitmentQueries", "module": "OpenItems",
 "of": "Commitments", "queries": ["open"],
 "accepts": ["Checked"], "conditional": [], "custody": "Frame",
 "decoder": null,
 "declaration": "sha256:...",
 "held_in": "anchors/<anchor-id>/consumers.metta",
 "changes_by": "A4"}

queries lists the observers of the machine named by of. accepts lists the statuses the consumer
acts on and conditional the solvers whose unreplayed answers it accepts; for EngineQueries
they are ["Checked", "Conditional"] and [{"solver": "cvc5"}]. decoder names the
map of the declaration’s Decode field and is held under the entry’s custody; for NagFollowUps

of Pattern 5 it is "follow-up-if-true". declaration is the interface digest of the declaration
together with everything it imports. For an agent consumer held_in names the component’s
own declaration file and changes_by is "gate".

C.2    Certificate store entry
One entry per result. The key is the request together with the four digests; a result is reused
only when all four match, or through the transport rules of Section 3, and an operation that
consumes it checks its binding first.
{"request": "(CheckUpdates WindowView)", "module": "OpenItems",
 "consumers": ["EngineQueries"],
 "interface": "sha256:...", "abstraction": "sha256:...",
 "code": "sha256:...", "registry": "sha256:...",
 "status": "Refuted",
 "binding": {"subject": ["WindowView", "window-view"], "machine": "GoalEngine",
             "states": "Live", "queries": {"suggest": "suggest"},
             "actions": {"tick": "tick", "reply": "on-reply"}, "merges": {},
             "tolerance": "exact", "scope": {"horizon": 1},
             "assumptions": [], "assurance": "Witness"},
 "evidence": {"witness": "update",
              "pair": [[2, "d0"], [2, "d2"]], "code_now": [2, "closed"],
              "action": "tick",
              "codes_after": [[3, "closed"], [3, "open"]]},
 "budget": {"limit": "(PairExpansions ...)", "used": "(PairExpansions ...)"},
 "conformance": {"corpus": "sha256:...", "result": "passed"},
 "issued": "..."}

interface is the digest of the module, abstraction that of the abstraction functions from live
records to model states, code that of the component the model describes, and registry that
of the registry with its patches. binding says what the result is about and under what it holds:
the subject declarations, the machine with its state carrier and the map behind each query,
action and merge label, the value tolerances, the scope (here one step from every state), the
undischarged assumptions, and the assurance, which is Exact for an exact method, Witness for
a replayed counterexample, and TrustedBackend or Assumed for a Conditional result, according
as its assumption is a solver’s unreplayed answer or one that a declaration carries. An Unknown
entry carries the work done in budget. conformance records the replay of Section 3.4; without
it, a reading of the result as a claim about the live component is Conditional on the abstraction
assumption, recorded as an (Assumption ...) entry that names the abstraction functions.

Each result carries the binding of its certificate store entry (Appendix C.2) together with the
entry’s module and interface digest. The gate compares digests with the evaluated surface,
checks that every module_digest is the evaluated module’s, and checks each binding against
each touched consumer (Section 12.3). Both results here are bound to the joint machine
AllThree. Its states and actions are those of Contra, Eff and Regen, and its queries include
each of their queries with the same map, so each result covers all three consumers by the
transport rule for removed queries. A result bound to Contra alone, such as (CheckBehavioral
CountsC), would cover ContraProbe and not EffectiveSupport: it was checked without the
query eff, so the gate would record it as InvalidInput for that consumer, name the missing
query, and count EffectiveSupport as inadequately covered.
    The gate then reads the status, assumptions and assurance of each covering result against
the consumer’s Accepts declaration. These two results are Checked by exhaustive enumeration
and carry no assumptions, which every consumer accepts. A result Conditional on cvc5
would count for a consumer only if its declaration named cvc5, as EngineQueries does and
EffectiveSupport does not; for the two frame consumers it would be inadequate coverage.
The same bundle for Pattern 3’s model of the cue-erosion repair, totals with the provenance of
contradicting incidents, would carry (CheckNow ProtectedE) as Refuted, with the witness pair
(0 0 2 0 0 0) and (0 1 1 0 0 0) and the answers 1 and 2, bound to Eff; it would block
promotion for EffectiveSupport. Estimated results go in estimated, each with its stream
digest and the level it spends. A goal change carries its CheckInterpretation result, bound
to the old and the new consumer declarations, both machines and both decoders with their
custody (Section 8.5).


D     Reproducing the results
The bundle mettadinc_omega_memory_goals_package.zip contains the module file memory_
goals.metta; the registry patch mg_registry_patch.json and the three patches it reuses; the
static checker ex_check.py; the Python models in models/, one file per pattern, with the small
library models/dclib.py; the reference evaluator mg_eval.py; the oracle mg_oracle.py and
its results mg_results.json; and the two streams of Pattern 8 in streams/. Python 3.11 or
later is assumed, with the standard library only.

MeTTaDinC                                            Omega memory and goals, 4 November 2026


The oracle. python3 mg_oracle.py runs 195 checks in about 68 seconds on one core, prints
one line per check, writes mg_results.json, and exits with a nonzero status if any check
fails. Its first part computes every result the patterns state from the Python models with
exact rational arithmetic. Its second part rebuilds every machine, representation, abstraction
map, block partition and implementation contract of the module from the module text with
mg_eval.py, reruns the checks on the rebuilt objects and compares them with the first part,
cell by cell where the patterns give a table. It also answers the module’s CheckInterpretation
requests, among them the two negative fixtures whose required status is InvalidInput, reads
every Consumer declaration, and applies the gate’s rule of Section 12.3 to bundles built from
the module’s results: a binding to another machine, a status or assumption the consumer does
not accept, and a Refuted result each get their required verdict.

The static check. The checker needs the specification’s validation package, which contains
the registry and the base checker:
MDINC_PACKAGE=/path/to/mettadinc_validation_package python3 ex_check.py \
    ex1_registry_patch.json ex2_registry_patch.json ex4_registry_patch.json \
    mg_registry_patch.json -- memory_goals.metta

The patches before – are applied in order. The first pass reports 10 modules, 318 declarations
and 92 run requests, all conforming to the registry with the patches; the second pass lists the
diagnostics against the unpatched registry, which are exactly the forms that depend on the
patches.

The reference evaluator. mg_eval.py reads the module with its own small reader and
evaluates what the module uses: Cases, Body and Table maps, parameterized maps and Switch,
the arithmetic and comparison primitives, Enum, Product, Subset and Reach carriers, the Like,
ActionsOf and For entries, the tolerance bodies OneMinusAbsoluteDifference, Pullback and
Meet, and implementation contracts with Pairs. It also reads Consumer declarations with their
Accepts clauses and decoders, answers CheckInterpretation, records the binding of a result,
and decides whether a bundle’s results cover each touched consumer. It is a test aid for this
guide and not an implementation of MeTTaDinC.

reproducing a detection means replaying the stored stream, not rerunning the generator. The
