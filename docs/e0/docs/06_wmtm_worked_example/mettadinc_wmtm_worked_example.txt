                                               Abstract
     Part II of this guide showed how to state the memory decisions of a live store as summary
     statistics, certificates that a solver checks for every state at once, and how to measure from
     logged receipts what no declaration can settle. This worked example applies the method end
     to end to one store, the open items from which WMTM’s goal engine proposes follow-ups,
     along the eight steps of Part II’s protocol. The data are simulated: eight weeks of the
     engine’s log-only phase, with 1,357 items, 955 replies, a reply detector that misses some of
     them, spurious closing notes, paraphrased questions, and two record designs running side by
     side.
         The declarations take three short modules. A solver establishes the event statistic and
     the store’s indexes for every timing and every store size, results reported as Conditional on
     that solver because its proofs are not yet checked; the window design, which closes items
     by what a recall window shows, is Refuted with replayable witnesses; and a replay of the
     frozen journal through the module’s maps agrees with the simulated designs on all 8,966
     daily decisions. The receipts needed more care than the protocol gave them. The engine
     proposes an open item every day, so one missed reply yields a run of stale proposals, and
     the example reduces the labels to replies, keys, key lifetimes, closings and audited items,
     each stream naming its population, selection rule and policy.
         A parser update on day 21 raises the detector’s miss rate from 1.5 to 25 percent. Part
     II’s mixture monitor, which accumulates evidence from the first receipt, never fires: three
     healthy weeks count as evidence for the accepted rate. A Shiryaev–Roberts changepoint
     monitor fires 47 hours after the update; replies audited after the alarm confirm the regression,
     and a rollback and re-parse restore all 18 lost replies. At the promotion gate, four claims
     from the reviewed receipts establish at level 0.01 that fewer than one reply in twenty is
     followed by a stale proposal, that fewer than one key in twenty is a duplicate and one closing
     in twenty is false, and that the event design errs on fewer items than the window design.
     The random audit that checks the review behind those receipts is still too small to establish
     its own two claims, and the gate promotes the engine on conditions it records with the
     claims. The next release makes the deployed engine index invalid input to the checker before
     any data are seen; its replacement is the coarsest the consumers allow, and every index can
     be rebuilt from the durable records.

1     Introduction
Part II of this guide carried Part I’s finite models of Omega’s memory and goals over to the live
system, where ages are real numbers, sources form an open set and stores grow without bound.
Its central object was the summary statistic: an encoding of the live state, with an abstract
update for every operation and a read map for every query, whose defining identities a solver
checks for all states at once. What no declaration can settle, such as how often the detector
that decides whether a message is a reply misses one, was to be estimated from logged receipts
with confidence sequences and watched with monitors that stay valid however often they are
read. Part II closed with a protocol of eight steps for applying all this to one store, and named
the store: WMTM’s open items and the goal engine, whose first live review prompted Part I [1,
Section 9].
    This document carries the protocol out. What I wanted from it was to see the method at
the size of a deployment, next to a journal and a review log, including the morning a monitor
fires, and to find out which parts of the protocol needed more than Part II gave them.

The data are simulated. The live journal, the engine’s suggestion log and the labels of
a live review were not available for this work. A seeded simulation stands in for them. It is
built from what Parts I and II and the WMTM report say about the store and the engine [5],
and its parameters were chosen to be plausible rather than measured from a running system.
Every number in this text comes from that simulation. The simulation writes its output once as
frozen streams pinned by SHA-256 digests, the module reads only the streams, and the oracle
regenerates them byte for byte from the seed. Running the same requests on streams frozen
from the live system needs no change to the declarations except the digests.

What happens in the eight weeks. During the engine’s log-only phase, its proposals are
logged and reviewed rather than shown in the prompt. Two record designs run side by side
on the same journal: the recall window that the engine used at its first review, and the event
statistic that Part II recommends. For three weeks nothing unusual happens. On the Monday
of the fourth week a parser update lowers the reply detector’s accuracy, and the share of replies
it misses rises from 1.5 to 25 percent. A changepoint monitor on the reply receipts raises an
alarm on the Wednesday morning, 47 hours after the update. On the Thursday morning the
team audits the replies received since the update; those received after the alarm confirm it, so
the team rolls the parser back at noon and re-parses the journal, which restores all 18 replies
the update had lost. The monitor restarts the next morning and stays quiet for the rest of the
eight weeks. At the end of the eighth week a promotion gate decides, from the receipts of the
last four weeks, whether the engine’s suggestions go into the prompt with the event design.
After the gate, the next release adds a snooze action and a three-day follow-up goal, and the
store is checked against it before it ships.

1.1    What the example adds to Part II
Most of the protocol works as Part II wrote it. Five points needed more than the protocol said.
    1. Receipts have to be reduced to units whose average is what a claim is about. The engine
       proposes an open item every day, so one missed reply produces up to seven stale proposals
       of the same item, and in the log-only phase the number of proposals depends on a policy
       that promotion changes. Part II warned about this [1, Section 5.2]; the example replaces
       per-proposal labels by five kinds of receipt, one per reply, per key, per key lifetime, per
       closing and per audited item, and each stream names its population, selection rule and
       policy (Section 4).

                                                4
MeTTaDinC                               Omega memory and goals, worked example, 4 November 2026


    2. A monitor whose evidence accumulates from the first receipt reacts late to a change
       that comes late. Part II’s mixture monitor never fires on the regression, because the
       healthy weeks before it count as evidence for the accepted rate. The example proposes a
       changepoint form of the same monitors, which needs two new arguments of one request,
       bounds its false alarms over any fixed horizon, and confirms its alarm on replies that the
       alarm did not use (Section 5).
    3. Every claim that the gate can establish from receipts rests on a review whose blind spots
       have to be named. The review of proposals cannot see false closings, so the example
       adds a review of closings; and the random audit, which checks the review independently,
       collects observations at a third and a sixth of the rate of the reply receipts and is not yet
       decisive after four weeks. Because the intervals are confidence sequences, the gate can
       report those claims now and again later without spending more of its confidence budget
       (Section 6).
    4. A release that adds an action makes the deployed engine index invalid input to the checker
       before any data are seen, and the coarsest replacement merges into one class every open
       item that a snooze has pushed past the engine’s seven days (Section 7).
    5. Keeping the event statistic in the durable records pays for itself at the first goal change.
       The rebuild of every index from the records is itself a summary statistic, which the solver
       establishes (Section 7.5).
The example also answers the question Part II left to it: how much of the stale-suggestion
rate is due to the record design, which the certified statistics remove, and how much to missed
replies, which only better detection reduces (Section 6.8).

1.2    Status
The three modules of the example conform to the registry together with the patches of the
worked examples [4], of Part I, of Part II, and of this example, which adds two arguments to one
request. The verdict of every symbolic request has been obtained twice: by the cvc5 solver [19],
on the obligations that Part II’s reference evaluator builds from the module text, and by the Z3
solver [18], on encodings written by hand; for a refuted request, both solvers find a witness. No
proof of unsatisfiability is checked, so under the specification’s assurance policy these verdicts
are Conditional on cvc5, the solver the reference evaluator calls, with Z3’s agreement recorded
beside them [3, Section 2.2], [1, Section 7.2]; the tables say Conditional for them. Verdicts
computed in exact arithmetic are Checked, and refutations are Refuted with witnesses replayed
by direct evaluation. Every estimate and monitor has been computed from the frozen streams
by the evaluator and by the Python models, and the evaluator refuses a stream whose content
no longer matches its declared digest; the oracle tests this on a copy of the receipts with every
label changed. No MeTTaDinC runtime exists yet. The deployment is simulated, and nothing
here describes a change made to a running Omega or WMTM system.


2     The deployment and its simulation
2.1    WMTM’s open items and the goal engine
WMTM (Working Medium-Term Memory) is the memory and reasoning layer of the Proto-
Cosmo2 agent. It keeps a journal of notes, and some notes carry explicit markers. A note that
begins “open question for Ben:” or “pending:” opens an item; a note marked “decided:” that
answers it closes it [5]. Deciding whether a note answers an open item is the job of a reply
detector in the parser, which matches the note to the item’s person and topic. Each item is

                                                 5
MeTTaDinC                              Omega memory and goals, worked example, 4 November 2026


filed under a deduplication key, the pair of the person it concerns and a topic label that the
parser assigns.
    The goal engine reads this store once a day. It proposes a follow-up for every item that
is open and at most seven days old, once per key. Whether its proposals reach the language
model’s prompt is controlled by a flag, goal_prompt in Part I’s notes [2, Section 4.5]. While
the flag is off, in the log-only phase, the proposals are written to a log, and a reviewer reads
the log and labels each proposal: stale if the item had already been answered, duplicate if
another key holds the same question. The reviewer also reads the thread of the first closing note
that memory records for each item and labels it spurious if the note did not answer the item.
Turning the flag on is a change to the running agent, and under GOLEM-Iter, the framework
under which such changes are tested and promoted, it passes a promotion gate: a decision point
at which the claims the change rests on are checked or estimated and recorded [6].
   Other readers use the same store. The agent’s commitment record asks whether an item is
open, whatever its age. A follow-up goal, here called the nag goal, asks whether an item is open
and at most two days old, so that the agent can follow up while the question is fresh. These
readers are the store’s consumers, and what the store must remember is decided by them.
     The first live review of the engine failed partly because “stale items stayed open (old replies
were missed)” and “paraphrases repeated” [5]. Part I traced the first failure, in a finite model,
to closing items by what the engine’s recall window shows: a reply that has left the window
makes an answered item look open again. Part II showed the same on real time [1, Section 6.1].
The log-only phase of this example therefore runs two record designs side by side on the same
journal. Under the window design, the first closing note the engine has learned of counts for
two days from the time it was learned, and the item looks open again afterwards; this is how
Part I modeled the engine at its first review. Under the event design, the item is closed from its
first closing note on. Both designs decide on every key at every daily cycle, and both proposal
lists are logged and reviewed.

2.2   The simulation
The simulation generates the journal one item at a time. Table 1 lists its parameters. Items are
opened at a Poisson rate of 30 a day on weekdays and 8 a day at weekends, at times spread
uniformly over working hours, from 08:00 to 20:00. Each concerns one of six people, with Ben
the most frequent at about one item in three, and gets a topic label made of one of 25 topic
stems and the item’s number. Seventy-two percent of items receive a reply, after a delay drawn
from a lognormal distribution with a median of 0.8 days; the spread is wide, so some replies
come within the hour and some after more than a week, at any hour of the day.
    Three kinds of input error corrupt what memory sees. The reply detector misses a reply
with probability 0.015 under parser version 1 and 0.25 under version 2, which is deployed on
day 21 at 10:00. A spurious closing note, a “decided:” note that the detector attaches to the
wrong item, arrives for 1.2 percent of items after an exponentially distributed delay with a
mean of 1.5 days. A paraphrase, a later note that asks the same question under a new topic
label, occurs for 0.6 percent of items within a day of the original, and it creates a second key. A
third of the items, chosen at random, are audited at the age of four days by a person who reads
the whole thread and records whether a reply had arrived and whether memory shows the item
as closed.
   The engine’s cycle runs at 09:00 every day. At each cycle both designs decide on every
key that is at most seven days old, and the review labels every proposal of either design and
the first closing of every key that memory learned of since the last cycle. Once the review is
done, the changepoint monitor of Section 5.4 reads one receipt for each reply received since the
previous cycle, in the order of the replies’ times. When it fires, the team follows a rule fixed in

then recovered with the probability that version 1 finds a reply. The monitor restarts at the

    The simulation keeps the ground truth, which replies arrived and which keys are duplicates,
beside the parsed journal. It uses the truth only to write the review’s labels and the audit’s
findings and to check the estimates afterwards; neither record design sees it. Two simplifications
should be kept in mind. The simulated review and audit make no mistakes, so every label is
correct, which in a live deployment is a hypothesis that the audit checks (Section 6.4). The
input errors are independent of each other by construction. The gate’s one-sided claims need
neither independence nor a common rate (Section 4.1), but a two-sided interval read as an
estimate of one rate, and the planning sizes of Section 6.5, assume both, and here they hold by
design. Neither the language model nor the content of the notes is modeled, since no consumer
of this store reads them.

2.3   The eight weeks
Table 2 gives the calendar. Over the eight weeks 1,357 items were opened, 1,218 of them on
weekdays, and the journal of the eight weeks holds 2,326 notes. The items produced 1,362 keys,
five of them duplicates created by paraphrases. Before day 56, 955 items received a reply; nine
received a spurious closing note; 438 were audited at age four. The event design logged 3,767
proposals, 67.3 a day on average, and the window design 7,176.

    The list decides the rest of the section. A statistic is sufficient only relative to the queries it

3.2    Step 2: the item machine and the abstraction function
The item machine is Part II’s ItemTime, imported unchanged. Its states are the triples (a, c, e)
with e ≤ a; waiting d days, for any d ≥ 0, adds d to the age and to the time since the closing
note, and a closing note sets c and starts that time at zero, unless the item was closed already.
Every claim of this section is about these declarations. Part II has checked its statistics on
them, and the example runs those requests again so that its bundle can be checked without
Part II’s.
   The abstraction function connects the machine to the journal. It reads the parsed notes in
time order and, for each note, does one of three things. A note parsed as an opening creates the
key of its person and topic label, with the note’s time as the creation time, if that key does not


                                                  8
MeTTaDinC                                  Omega memory and goals, worked example, 4 November 2026


exist yet. A note parsed as closing an item applies the action reply to the item of its key at the
time memory learns of the note: the note’s own time when the note is parsed as it is written,
and the time of the re-parse for a reply that a re-parse recovers. Later closing notes of the same
key change nothing, and notes parsed as anything else are ignored. Between these events, every
item waits. A note cannot change a decision made before memory learned of it, which is why
the time of learning, and not the note’s timestamp, is the time of the action. The abstraction
function is code under test, as Part II says [1, Section 8.3], and its errors are the input errors
of Section 2.2: a reply the detector parses as an ordinary note, a closing note attached to the
wrong item, and a paraphrase that receives a new topic label and therefore a new key.

3.4    Step 4: the store, durable records and indexes

statistics are declared on it, and Part II’s lifting proposition [1, Proposition 4.3] carries each
item statistic over to the store, with its status and assumptions: the identities hold item by
item, and a keyed store of any size is a finite map from keys to items whose operations act
on one key, or on all keys alike for the global wait. The proposition has a premise that the
lifting request checks before it lifts anything: the item statistic must be a statistic of the store’s
item machine, ItemTime, whose consumers ask both suggest and open. The nag index is a
statistic of NagTime, which has the same items and actions and asks only nag, so its declaration
names that machine as a view with (View NagTime), and the lifted index is bound to the query
nag alone. Without the view the declaration would be refused as invalid input, since an index
certified for one query says nothing about the others.

3.5     Conformance replay
The verdicts of Table 5 are about declarations. Whether the simulated designs behave as the
declarations say is a separate question, which Part I answers by replaying recorded events
through the code and through the declared machine [2, Section 3.4]. The oracle does this for
the whole deployment, starting from the frozen journal. It reads the journal through its own
implementation of the abstraction function described above and checks that the result is the
simulation’s 1,362 keys, with their creation times and the times from which they are closed.
It then steps the module’s item machine, the event statistic, the joint statistic and the nag
index through each key’s history, evaluating the module’s maps as the reference evaluator reads
them. At every daily cycle at which the key was at most seven days old, it compares what
the statistics decide, and what the module’s window view shows, with the decisions of the two
simulated designs. All 8,966 decisions agree, and at every cycle the code of each statistic equals
the encoding of the replayed item, which is identity (U) observed on the data.
    The replay covers every decision of this corpus, so no sampling bound is needed for it. Both
sides of the comparison receive the same events once the journal has been abstracted, so the
replay tests the designs’ code against the module’s maps. It does not test the abstraction
function itself: the parser, the detector and the topic labeler reach the claims of this example
only through the receipts of the next section.

[1, Section 9]. The example keeps the random audit as the protocol describes it. It replaces the

4.1   Why single proposals are the wrong unit
The engine proposes an open item at every cycle until the item is closed or older than seven
days. A reply that the detector misses therefore produces a stale proposal at every cycle from
the reply to the item’s seventh day, so one input error yields a run of labels. Kofi’s item of
Table 4 was proposed at the cycles of days 19 to 24, and the last two proposals were stale,
because the reply of day 22 had been missed. In the gate window the event design logged 1,952
proposals, of which the review labeled 39 stale and 30 duplicate. The 39 stale labels came from
7 keys, two of them stale on seven consecutive days, and the 30 duplicate labels from 5 keys.
    Part II estimates rates with a mixture confidence sequence, in the line that begins with
Robbins [15] and whose general theory Howard and coauthors give [16]. Read as an estimate of a
rate, its interval assumes that every observation has one common conditional rate given the ones
before it [1, Section 5.1]. Labels that come in runs from one key break that assumption: once a
key has been proposed stale, its next proposal is stale too unless the key ages out, so an interval
computed from the 1,952 labels as if they shared one rate would be too narrow. A one-sided
claim, that the rate is below a target, needs no common rate: by Part II’s Proposition 5.1 it
holds for the average, over the observations, of each one’s conditional probability given those
before it [1, Proposition 5.1]. Even so, the proposals are the wrong unit for the gate’s claims,
because that average counts a missed reply once for every day its item stays young, and the
number of days depends on the log-only policy. Once the engine is live, an answer of “already
handled” closes the item at its first stale proposal, so a rate per logged proposal does not carry
over to the live engine. The per-proposal labels are kept, as the frozen stream proposals, and
this text quotes them only as descriptions. For comparison, the window design logged 3,752
proposals in the same four weeks, of which 1,839 were stale.
    Kofi’s two stale proposals become one observation at the unit used below. His reply produced
a single reply receipt, released at the cycle of day 23 and labeled stale, and it was one of the
receipts that raised the alarm of Section 5.4.

    A lifetime receipt is written for each key that is not a duplicate and that at least one design
proposed, when its seven days end. It carries one label per design, which is 1 when the design
erred on the key at some cycle: it proposed the item although the item had been answered, or
it did not propose the item while the item was open and the other design proposed it. The
second clause records a missed follow-up in the only way the log can show one, through the
other design’s proposal. The two labels of a row concern the same key at the same cycles, so
the rows are pairs, and Part II’s comparison of two policies on the same snapshots applies [1,
Section 5.4]. That comparison is a sequential form of McNemar’s test for paired proportions
[17]: only the keys on which exactly one design erred carry information about which design errs
more, and the claim that the window design errs more often is one-sided, so Proposition 5.1
of Part II covers it as well. The stream LivesGate keeps the receipts of the keys whose daily
decisions all fall in the gate window, which are the keys created after the cycle of day 27 whose

the window into it, including some from the days of the regression. An error that both designs
make at the same cycle, such as withholding an item that both see as closed, leaves no trace in
these labels; the next unit is the one that sees it.
    A closure receipt is written for each key that is not a duplicate at the first cycle after memory
learned of its first closing, and carries the review’s label: spurious when no reply had arrived by
the time of the closing note. Under the event design the first closing decides the item for good,
so the closure receipts measure, closing by closing, the false closings that the engine inherits.

had a reply by then, the label says whether memory still shows it open; for an item without a
reply, whether memory shows it closed. The two kinds go to two streams, because they estimate

4.3   Where the labels come from
In a live deployment the labels have different sources, and each source has something it cannot
see. A reply the detector found is known to memory. A reply it missed becomes known when
the reviewer labels the item’s next proposal stale and finds the reply in the thread; the reply
receipt is then written with the reply’s time and the label 1. A missed reply that the reviewer
also overlooks produces no receipt at all, so the reply streams undercount misses at the rate at
which the review overlooks them. The random audit is the independent check, since its sample
is fixed before anyone knows how the item will go and the auditor reads the whole thread.
    False closings are invisible to the review of proposals, because an item closed by a spurious
note is never proposed by the event design again. The review of closings sees them, since it reads
the thread of every first closing. Ben’s question about token-bridge-0288 is the example. The
event design proposed it at the cycle of day 11, before the spurious note arrived that evening,
and never again. The review of closings labeled the note spurious at the cycle of day 12; in the
log-only phase nothing feeds a label back to memory, so the item stayed closed under the event
design. The window design proposed it again on days 14 to 17, once the note’s two days in the
window had passed, and for this item that was the right decision. The item was also in the
random sample, and its audit on day 14, at the age of four days, recorded it as open in the
thread and closed in memory. A false closing is the one failure that the event design makes
worse than the window design. The window design stops counting a closing note after two days,
so a false closing costs it at most two days of proposals, while the event design keeps the closing
until someone corrects it.
   Over the eight weeks the random audit found one false closing among 159 audited items
without a reply, and six missed replies among 279 audited items with one. The review of closings
labeled three first closings spurious: Ben’s, and two in the gate window.

however long the monitor runs [13, 14]. This α is the level of the guarantee, the probability
of error it allows, and every estimate in this example is stated at the same level: an interval
at level 0.01 fails to contain the true rate with probability at most 0.01. The second Detect
request is the changepoint scheme of Section 5.4, which the deployment ran in the loop. The
remaining requests are the estimates of Section 5.5.

5.2   What happened
Parser version 2 went live on day 21, a Monday, at 10:00. It changed how the reply detector
matched a “decided:” note to an open item, and in the simulation it misses a reply with
probability 1/4, against 0.015 for version 1. Nothing in the declarations changed. The detector
is part of the abstraction function, outside every declaration, and the statistics went on answering
correctly for the events they were given, so the change showed only in the receipts. Receipt 362
was the last for a reply received before the update; of the 37 receipts from receipt 363 to
receipt 399, released at the cycles of days 22 and 23, ten were stale.

5.3   Why a monitor started at the first receipt stays silent
Part II’s monitor never fired. Its value after the first receipt, 0.807, was the largest it reached
over the whole stream. By receipt 362 the logarithm of its value had fallen to −16.93; the alarm
needs the logarithm to reach ln 100 = 4.61; and by receipt 399, after the ten stale receipts, it
stood at −11.46. After the rollback it fell again, to −31.2 at the last of the 916 receipts.
    Each receipt that is not stale multiplies every monitor’s product by (1 − q)/(1 − p0 ) < 1, so
three healthy weeks leave every product, and hence their average, far below one. A change that
comes after them must first make up that factor before the monitor can count any evidence
against the accepted rate. The monitor answers the question it was built for, whether the
stream since the first receipt is consistent with the accepted rate, and after 362 healthy receipts
a few dozen bad ones take a long time to change that answer. Part II’s simulations of monitors
used streams whose rate was the same from the first receipt on [1, Section 5.5], where this does
not arise.
    A counterfactual run of the same simulation without the rollback shows the cost. With
version 2 left in place, Part II’s monitor would have fired at receipt 524 on day 30, a week
after the changepoint monitor. On days 24 to 30 the event design would have logged 169 stale
proposals, where the run with the rollback logged 21: the late alarm would have cost 148 stale
proposals.

5.4    A changepoint monitor
The statistics of quickest detection address this case: observations follow one law until an
unknown time and another afterwards, and an alarm should come soon after the change and
rarely before it. Page’s cumulative sum [7] and the procedure of Shiryaev and Roberts [8, 9] are
the classical answers, with optimality results by Lorden [10] and Pollak [11]. Shin, Ramdas and
Rinaldo built such procedures from e-processes, nonnegative processes whose expected value
under the null hypothesis is at most one at every stopping time [12]. That construction extends
them to composite null hypotheses, which allow a range of rates, such as “the rate is at most
p0 ”, instead of a single one. The example uses the Shiryaev–Roberts form. For each alternative
q, let R0 = 0 and

The term for k is the likelihood ratio of “the rate became q at receipt k” against “no change”,
over the receipts from k to n, so Rn adds the evidence for every possible start of a change. The
term for k = 1 is the product of Part II’s monitor. The mixture R̄n averages Rn over the three
alternatives, and the alarm is raised at the first n with R̄n ≥ A.

Proposition 5.1 (False alarms of the changepoint monitor). Let X1 , X2 , . . . be binary labels
such that, for every n, the conditional probability that Xn = 1 given X1 , . . . , Xn−1 is at most
                                     (i)
p0 . Let q1 , . . . , qm > p0 , let Rn be the statistic above for qi , R̄n their average, and T the first n
with R̄n ≥ A, for some A > 0. Then
   1. E[T ] ≥ A, and
   2. P (T ≤ N ) ≤ N/A for every N ≥ 1.

Proof. Write pn for the conditional probability that Xn = 1 given the past. The conditional
expectation of the factor for qi is pn qi /p0 + (1 − pn )(1 − qi )/(1 − p0 ), which increases with
pn because qi > p0 and equals one at pn = p0 , so it is at most one. Hence the conditional
                 (i)                 (i)
expectation of Rn is at most 1 + Rn−1 , and the same holds for the average: the conditional
expectation of R̄n is at most 1 + R̄n−1 .
    For the first part, R̄n − n is then a supermartingale that starts at zero: its conditional
expectation, given the past, never exceeds its last value. For every k the stopping time T ∧ k is
bounded, and optional stopping gives E[R̄T ∧k ] ≤ E[T ∧ k]. Since R̄ is nonnegative and at least
A at time T , the left side is at least A P (T ≤ k). Letting k grow gives E[T ] ≥ A P (T < ∞). If
P (T < ∞) = 1 this is the claim, and otherwise E[T ] is infinite; hence E[T ] ≥ A.
    For the second part, fix N and let Mn = R̄n +(N −n) for n = 0, . . . , N . Then M0 = N , every
Mn is nonnegative, and the conditional expectation of Mn is at most 1 + R̄n−1 + N − n = Mn−1 ,
so M is a nonnegative supermartingale. Ville’s inequality bounds the probability that it ever
reaches A by M0 /A = N/A. On the event T ≤ N , MT ≥ R̄T ≥ A. Hence P (T ≤ N ) ≤ N/A.

In plain terms. A monitor that adds up the evidence for a change starting at every possible
receipt will raise a false alarm sooner or later, but not soon. With the threshold at 100,000, it
reads at least 100,000 receipts on average before its first false alarm when nothing has changed,
and the chance of a false alarm within any N receipts is at most N in 100,000.
    The guarantee is of a different kind from Ville’s. Ville’s inequality bounds the probability of
any false alarm over an unbounded future. The proposition bounds the expected number of
receipts before a false alarm, and its probability over any fixed number of receipts, but not over
an unbounded future: if each receipt is stale with some positive probability, independently of

MeTTaDinC                                                             Omega memory and goals, worked example, 4 November 2026


the others, the stream contains, with probability one, a run of stale receipts long enough to
carry R̄n past any threshold. The ability to detect a late change is paid for with false alarms
at long intervals, at least A receipts apart on average if the monitor restarts after each. An
alarm is then a reason to look, and the look has to produce evidence that the alarm did not use,
which is what the confirmation rule of Section 5.5 requires. The deployment set A = 100,000.
At 16.4 receipts a day that is at least 16.7 years of receipts, on average, before a false alarm.
By the second part, the probability of a false alarm was at most 0.0092 over the 916 receipts of
the eight weeks, and it is at most 0.06 over a year of receipts at the same rate. The detection
delay of such procedures grows only with the logarithm of A [10, 11]. Declaring this monitor
needs two new arguments of Detect, the scheme and the mean time to a false alarm, which the
registry patch of Appendix B adds.

6.1   What the gate decides
One candidate arrives at the gate: goal_prompt turns on, and the engine decides from the
store’s engine index, the joint statistic lifted to the store, instead of from its recall window.
The candidate changes what the engine reads and nothing else, so it touches one consumer,
the engine’s queries. Part I declared that consumer with the agent’s custody: the agent may
propose changes to it, and each change is a candidate that passes the gate. Consumers with the

                                                  21
MeTTaDinC                              Omega memory and goals, worked example, 4 November 2026


frame’s custody, such as the commitment record, are held outside the agent’s authority and
change only through an external ceremony [2, Section 3.5]. GOLEM-Iter sorts candidates into
authority classes by the most authority they could plausibly need, and a change to a persistent
memory policy, such as this one, is class A2 [2, Section 2.3].
    The candidate arrives with a certificate bundle: the CheckStatistic results for the touched
consumer, and the Estimated rates on which its empirical claims rest. Every result in the bundle
is bound to the machine and queries it was checked against, and the gate compares that binding,
the result’s status and its assumptions with the consumer’s declaration. The symbolic results are
Conditional on cvc5. The engine’s queries are an operational consumer with the agent’s custody,
and their declaration accepts Checked results and results whose only assumption is the cvc5
backend, (Accepts Checked (Conditional (TrustedBackend cvc5))); a consumer with the
frame’s custody would need the solver’s proofs checked first [2, Section 3.5], [1, Section 7.2].
The gate has a lifetime confidence budget, the total probability of error it allows itself over all
its decisions. Certificates spend nothing from it, whether Checked or Conditional on a solver,
and each Estimated result spends its level [2, Section 12.3], [1, Section 8.3]. Part I’s gate rule is
that a Refuted result for a touched consumer blocks promotion and that Estimated results with
too little data behind them are a reason to keep the candidate in shadow, where it runs beside
the deployed version without affecting what users see. The claims, their targets and the gate
window were fixed before day 28.

6.4   The decision
The candidate is promoted on conditions. goal_prompt goes on, and the engine decides from
the store’s engine index. The certificates cover the touched consumer with a status it accepts,
nothing is Refuted, and E1, E2, E3 and E6 are established.
    The candidate’s absolute error budget is one in twenty for each kind of error the engine can
make: a stale proposal after a reply, which E1 bounds; a duplicate key, which E2 bounds; and
a follow-up withheld from an open item, which E6 bounds. Under the event design an open
item at most seven days old goes unproposed only when a closing note has closed it, and a
closing note on an open item is a spurious closing, so E6 is the bound on withheld follow-ups.
E3 is a comparison. It shows that the event design errs on fewer keys than the window design,
which bounds no absolute harm: a design can beat a poor comparator and still miss too many
follow-ups. The decision therefore rests on E1, E2 and E6 for the budget, and on E3 only for
the choice between the two designs.

6.5   Planning the audit
Part II’s planning table gives the number of observations at which the upper end of the interval
falls below a target when the observed rate equals the true rate [1, Section 5.6]. That is a
plug-in size. At the rates the simulation used it is 521 audited replies for E4 and 414 audited
open items for E5, and at those sizes the claims would be established only 63 and 68 times in a
hundred, because the observed count varies around its mean. A binomial calculation gives the
sizes from which on each claim is established with probability at least 0.9: 776 audited replies
and 587 audited open items. The gate window yielded 35 audited replies and 19 audited open
items a week, so the audit streams reach those sizes about 22 and 31 weeks after the start of
the gate window. Auditing every item at the age of four days, instead of a third, would cut
both times to a third. A team planning this in a live deployment would use a pessimistic rate
instead of the simulation’s, and the times would be longer. For comparison, bounding the reply
receipts’ rate below 3/100 instead of 1/20 with the same probability would take 3,304 receipts,
about 30 weeks at the gate window’s rate.

6.7   The confidence budget
The six Estimated claims spend 6 × 0.01 = 0.06 of the gate’s lifetime budget; the certificates
and the conformance replay spend nothing. The three estimates of the incident in Section 5.5
are not gate claims, and a union bound over all nine estimates of the example bounds by 0.09
the probability that any of their intervals fails to contain its true rate.
    Reading E4 and E5 again at a later gate costs nothing more. Each interval is a confidence
sequence, valid at every row of its stream simultaneously, so if the audit streams are extended
rather than restarted and the claims stay the same, the later reading is covered by the 0.01
already spent. This is the practical reason for anytime-valid estimates at a gate: E4 and E5 are
reported now as not yet established, and the report that establishes them, if it comes, needs no
new charge.

7     The next release
After the gate the product side asks for two changes. The first is a snooze: on a proposal,
“remind me in s days” keeps the engine from proposing the item for s days. The second extends
the nag goal from two days to three. Both are memory decisions, and Part II’s notes on Pattern 5
say what to do before such a release ships: check the classification of the goal change and
check the store’s statistics against the new goal set [1, Section 6.5]. The module WMTMRelease
declares the new item machine and every statistic the release needs, and its requests answer
both questions before any data from the release exist.

7.1   The item with a snooze
An item now has a fourth component, the days r of snooze left. Waiting d days lowers r to
max(0, r − d). A snooze of s days sets r to max(r, s), so a second snooze can extend the first
but never shorten it. The engine proposes an item when it is open, at most seven days old and
not snoozed, ¬c ∧ a ≤ 7 ∧ r ≤ 0. The commitment record’s query is unchanged, and the two
nag goals read the age as before.

7.2    The deployed engine index is invalid input
JointCarried is the engine index of the deployed store, carried over to the new machine with
its steps for waiting and replies and nothing for the snooze. The checker answers InvalidInput
and names the missing step, before posing any obligation: a summary statistic must have an
abstract update for every action family of its machine [1, Definition 3.1], and this one has none
for the snooze. The status says that the declaration is not a statistic of the new machine. The
request CheckNow CarriedIndex, on the same encoding declared as a representation of the
new machine, shows that no step could be added to make it one. It is Refuted with the witness
x = (0, false, 0, 1) and y = (0, false, 0, 0): two open items of age zero, the first snoozed for a day,
which have the same code and different answers to the engine’s query. A store that went on
serving the engine from the deployed index after the release would therefore propose snoozed
items, and the two requests establish this from the declarations alone, before a single snooze
has been logged.

It is Conditional on cvc5 with eight obligations: closure and update for waiting, replies and

any snooze so far.
    JointS is the engine index of the release. It keeps three kinds of value: closed; open but
out of the engine’s reach for good; and live, with its age and its snooze. An open item is out
of reach when a + r > 7, because by the time its snooze ends it will be past seven days. The
statistic is Conditional on cvc5 with eight obligations, and it is the coarsest for the engine and
the commitment record together.

a query, that separates them. Two live codes with different snoozes are separated by waiting
out the shorter snooze, after which only one of the two is proposed. Two live codes with the
same snooze and different ages are separated by waiting until the younger is seven days old. A
live code and the out-of-reach class are separated by waiting out the live item’s snooze, and

the out-of-reach class stays out of reach under every wait and every snooze. The commitment

In plain terms. The engine index of the release keeps the age and the snooze of an open
item for as long as the item can still come back to the engine, and nothing more once it cannot.
Any two items that it stores differently are, at some later moment, treated differently by the
engine or by the commitment record, so no smaller index serves both.
    The out-of-reach class merges open items that differ in age and in snooze, and its members
include items that a person snoozed in good faith. An item five days old that is snoozed for
three days leaves the engine for good, while the commitment record still holds it open. The
randomized test of the oracle met such states in its runs. Whether that is what “remind me in
three days” should mean is a product question, and the declarations raise it before the release
ships. The release can accept it, cap a snooze at 7 − a days, or extend the engine’s query to
snoozed items past seven days, which would be a goal change to classify as in Part II’s Pattern 5.

7.5   Rebuilding the indexes from the records
The release has to replace the two-day index by the three-day index for every key that exists
when it ships. At that moment, items between two and three days old have lost their ages in
the two-day index, which is exactly what the three-day goal reads. The durable record kept
them, and the module states the rebuild as a summary statistic. The machine NewIndexView
has the actions of the item and one query, the three-day index itself. The record is a statistic of
that machine whose read map, rebuild-three, is the rebuild: identity (R) says that rebuilding
the record of an item gives the item’s index, and (C) and (U) say that the record keeps pace
with every action. RebuildFromRecords is Conditional on cvc5 with seven obligations.
    Part II’s Proposition 3.2 then applies twice. Applied to RebuildFromRecords, it gives that
after every history the rebuild of the stored record equals the three-day index of the item’s state.
Applied to NagThreeIndex, whose update identity holds for every action, the same argument
gives that the index maintained step by step from the item’s creation is also the three-day index
of the item’s state. The rebuilt index is therefore the index the key would have if the three-day
index had been maintained from the start. RebuildEngineIndex states and checks the same
for the engine index of the release, with JointS in the place of NagThreeIndex. Only indexes
that are functions of the stored age, closed flag and snooze left can be rebuilt this way.

In plain terms. Rebuilding the three-day index from the stored timestamps gives every

The release changes the engine’s query, so it is a new candidate at the gate, and the certificates
of Table 10 are its static evidence. The three claims of the gate’s absolute budget carry over
to it without new data, each by an argument of its own. The new query asks for everything
the old one asked and one more condition, so on the same journal the release can only remove
proposals: every reply receipt that is stale under the release was stale before, and since the
upper end of E1’s interval rises with the count of stale receipts, it remains an upper bound for
the release’s rate under the same assumptions. E6 concerns the records and does not depend
on the query. E2 is about keys at their first proposals, and these do not change. A snooze
is given only on a proposal, so until a key’s first proposal its snooze left is zero and the two

MeTTaDinC                              Omega memory and goals, worked example, 4 November 2026


queries agree: every key is first proposed at the same cycle as before, and KeysGate has the
same rows and labels under the release. The oracle checks this on the frozen journal. It steps
every key through the deployed engine index and through the release’s, giving a snooze of one
to four days on a random 30 percent of the release’s proposals, 900 snoozes in all. All 1,362
keys are first proposed at the same cycle as in the deployment, while the snoozes remove 411 of
the 1,952 proposals of the gate window.
    The argument for E2 rests on a detail of the product design. A release that let a person
snooze an item before the engine proposes it, from the item’s page say, could keep a key from
being proposed at all, and the keys the engine proposes would then be a selection that depends
on the policy. In the extreme case, a policy that withheld every key that is not a duplicate would
propose only the five duplicate keys of the gate window: a subset of the log-only proposals,
with a share of duplicates of five in five instead of five in 453. A rate bounded on one policy’s
selection of keys says nothing about another’s, so such a release would need new key receipts,
or a declared argument, before E2 could be claimed for it.
    What is new in this release is a way to fail that the declarations exclude and an implemen-
tation might not: proposing an item before its snooze ends. The replay of Section 3.5, run on
the release’s log with the release’s maps, is the check for that, as it was for the first promotion.


8     What the example teaches implementers
8.1   Declarations
The declarations of the store fill three modules in a file of 357 lines, with 33 requests. Four of
the requests rerun checks that Part II made on the declarations the example imports from it,
two lift Part II’s statistics to the new store, and the other 27 are new. The declarations did not
change during the incident, and their certificates were as valid on day 23 as on day 0, because
the regression was in the input events and every statistic is sufficient relative to the events it
is given. The checking happened at two moments, when the store was declared and when the
release was planned, and the whole oracle, with every check of this text, runs in about twelve
seconds on one core. An implementer should expect this distribution of effort: the declarations
are short and stable, and most of the work goes into the receipts.

8.2   Receipts
Receipts should be designed before they are collected. For each claim, choose the unit whose
average is what the claim is about, write the receipt at a moment fixed in advance, and give
it labels that the unit’s own events decide. A one-sided claim then holds for the average of
the units’ conditional rates, with no assumption of independence or of a common rate; reading
the same interval as an estimate of one rate needs both assumptions in addition. For the goal
engine that meant replies, keys, key lifetimes, first closings and audited items, and it meant
keeping the per-proposal labels only as descriptions. Release the receipts of a cycle together,
in an order that does not depend on their labels. Give every stream its population, selection
rule and policy, since an estimate made under one policy carries over to another only with an
argument; Section 7.6 carries the gate’s claims over to the release, each with its own argument,
and shows how a snooze given before the first proposal would break the argument for E2. For
each source of labels, write down what it cannot see: the review of proposals misses every false
closing, since a falsely closed item is never proposed, the lifetime labels miss an error that both
designs make at once, and the random audit sees memory at one age only and accumulates
slowly. Part I’s receipt formats [2, Appendix C.4] should therefore gain reply, key, lifetime,
closure and audit receipts next to the suggestion receipt.

MeTTaDinC                             Omega memory and goals, worked example, 4 November 2026


8.3   Monitors
A monitor on a live system should be a changepoint monitor. A process started at the first
observation tests whether a stream has been consistent with the accepted rate all along, and
after weeks of health it reacts late to a change, as Section 5.3 showed. The Shiryaev–Roberts
form gives up the bound on the probability of any false alarm and offers instead a bound on the
mean time to one and on its probability within any fixed number of receipts. That is acceptable
when an alarm leads to a confirmation on data the alarm did not use, as the audit of the replies
received after the alarm did here. The response to an alarm, the confirmation rule and the
action it licenses, should be fixed before the monitor runs, and the monitor restarted once the
repair is in place.

8.4   Gates
Each Estimated claim at a gate rests on the receipts it counts and on hypotheses about where
their labels come from. The bundle should record those hypotheses with the claims and name
the independent check of each; here the hypothesis was that the review finds the errors it labels,
and the random audit was its check. The decision to promote before the check is decisive is a
judgment the bundle should record, together with the conditions under which it is reversed.
State the absolute error budget beside every comparative claim, since a comparison with a poor
design bounds no absolute harm. The sizes a claim needs should be planned before the gate
window opens, for a stated probability of success rather than at the plug-in size. Here they
showed that a four-week window with a one-third audit cannot establish a target of one in
twenty for the audit’s rates. Because the intervals are confidence sequences, the audit’s claims
can be carried from one gate to the next on the level already spent.

8.5   Releases and durable records
Every release that changes the store’s actions or its consumers should rerun CheckStatistic
on the release’s machine before it ships. An InvalidInput for a missing step says that an
index must be extended or replaced, and a CheckNow witness on its encoding says when it must
be replaced. Here the deployed engine index had to be replaced, and the two-day nag index
needed only a step for the snooze, one that leaves it unchanged. The coarsest replacement
can surface product decisions, such as the snoozed items that leave the engine for good. The
durable records should keep the event statistic and what the release’s actions add to it, here the
latest end of any snooze, because only indexes that are functions of the record can be rebuilt
from it. Stating the rebuild as a statistic of the machine that observes the index turns “the
index can be rebuilt from the records” into a claim that a solver decides.


9     Limits
The parameters of Table 1 are plausible choices and not measurements of WMTM. The simulated
review and audit make no mistakes, and the input errors are independent by construction. In a
live deployment reviewers overlook things, and misses can be correlated, by topic or by person.
Correlated misses leave the gate’s one-sided claims valid, since these hold for the average of
the receipts’ conditional rates, but they make the two-sided intervals too narrow when these
are read as estimates of one rate, and they make the planning sizes, which assume one rate, a
rough guide.
   The gate’s four established claims rest on the review. A missed reply that the reviewer also
overlooks produces no receipt, so the reply streams undercount misses at the review’s own miss


                                               33
MeTTaDinC                             Omega memory and goals, worked example, 4 November 2026


rate, and the same holds for duplicates and false closings that the review does not recognize.
The audit bounds that effect only as fast as it accumulates, and Section 6.4 records the decision
to promote before the audit is decisive.
   The changepoint monitor’s guarantee bounds the mean time to a false alarm and the
probability of one over a fixed number of receipts, not over an unbounded future. It assumes
the conditional rate bound of this example’s Proposition 5.1 for the receipts in the order in
which the monitor reads them; the receipts of a cycle are released in the order of the replies’
times, which does not depend on their labels.
   The planning sizes of Section 6.5 use the simulation’s rates. A live team does not know the
true rates; it plans with pessimistic ones, and the times become longer.
   The parser, the reply detector and the topic labeler form the abstraction function, which is
code under test and which the conformance replay does not test; the language model is outside
the store altogether. Their behavior enters only through receipts, as in Parts I and II.
    The monitors average over three declared alternatives. A continuous mixture over all
rates above the accepted one, which Part II computed in Python, cannot yet be declared [1,
Section 10].
    The symbolic verdicts are Conditional on cvc5. Z3 agrees with each of them on encodings
written by hand, which is evidence and not a proof, and no proof is checked; Part II discusses
which of these verdicts could be backed by checked proofs today [1, Section 7.2]. The engine’s
queries accept results of this kind, and a consumer with the frame’s custody would not.


10     Conclusion
The store at the center of this example is small: a few timestamps per key and the indexes
computed from them. A solver established once, for every state the store can reach, that the
store answers what its consumers ask, and established it again for the release before it shipped.
The parser, the detector and the reviewer, which no solver reaches, were measured from receipts
at units chosen so that each claim bounds an average that keeps its meaning once the engine is
live. A changepoint monitor watched those receipts, and an audit of replies that the alarm had
not used confirmed its alarm before anyone acted on it. Three of the five points at which the
example needed more than Part II’s protocol concern the receipts, the monitor and the gate;
the other two concern releases, which the protocol did not cover. The example also answered
the question Part II left open: in the gate window the record design accounts for 1,800 of the
window design’s 1,839 stale proposals, and the input events for the other 39.
    I find it encouraging that the declarations needed no change once they were written. The
incident left every certificate valid, and the release added certificates without touching the old
ones. The distinction calculus asks of every memory which distinctions it must keep and for
whom; for this store that question took three short modules and a few seconds of solver time to
answer, and most of the effort went into the receipts. The next step is to run the same requests
on streams frozen from the live system, where the review makes mistakes and the parameters
are no longer chosen.

D     Reproducing the results
The bundle mettadinc_wmtm_example_package.zip contains the module file wmtm_example.
metta and the template wmtm_example.template.metta from which it is written once the
stream digests are known; Part II’s module file live_memory.metta and Part I’s memory_goals.
metta, which it imports from; the registry patch wx_registry_patch.json and the five patches
it builds on; the static checker ex_check.py; the simulation models/wx_sim.py, the derived
facts models/wx_facts.py, the Z3 encodings models/wx_smt.py and Part II’s confidence
sequences models/cs.py; the reference evaluator ls_eval.py; the oracle wx_oracle.py and
its results wx_results.json; the script make_module.py; and the twelve frozen streams in
streams/. Python 3.11 or later is assumed, with the packages numpy, scipy, z3-solver and cvc5;
the results were produced with numpy 2.5.3, scipy 1.18.1, z3-solver 5.3.3 and cvc5 1.4.2.

The oracle. python3 wx_oracle.py runs 99 checks in about 13 seconds on one core, prints
one line per check, writes wx_results.json, and exits with a nonzero status if any check fails.
Its first part (55 checks) regenerates the simulated deployment from its seed, writes the twelve
streams again into a temporary directory and compares them byte for byte with the shipped ones,
and recomputes the numbers of the text: the counts of the run, the regression, its confirmation
and the two monitors, a counterfactual run without the rollback, the estimates with Part II’s
confidence sequences, the ground truth against which they are checked, the decomposition of the
stale proposals, the planning figures, and the symbolic obligations of the example’s statistics in
Z3 on encodings written by hand. Its second part (44 checks) reads the three module files with
ls_eval.py, answers all 33 requests of the example from the module text, with cvc5 for the
symbolic ones and from the pinned streams for the others, replays every witness by evaluation,
compares each verdict and number with the first part, runs four differential tests, which are the
conformance replay of Section 3.5 from the frozen journal, the randomized test of the snooze
semantics, the rebuild of Section 7.5 and the replay of the journal through the release’s engine
index of Section 7.6, and checks that a copy of RepliesGate changed under its declared digest
is refused. Symbolic verdicts are reported as Conditional on cvc5. The oracle checks that Z3
reaches the same verdict on every obligation and records that agreement under agreed_by
beside each Conditional verdict in the section verdicts of wx_results.json.

The first pass reports 19 modules, 575 declarations and 162 run requests for the three files, of
which 3 modules, 85 declarations and 33 run requests are the example’s, all conforming to the
registry with the patches. The second pass lists the diagnostics against the unpatched registry,
which are exactly the forms that depend on the patches.

The reference evaluator. ls_eval.py is Part II’s evaluator, which checks the premise of
every lift, the target of every safety transfer and the digest of every stream before it computes
anything, and reports each verdict with its assurance, with one extension: Detect accepts the
Scheme and MeanTimeToFalseAlarm arguments of the patch, computes the Shiryaev–Roberts
statistics of the listed monitors in exact rational arithmetic, averages them, and reports the
first observation at which the average reaches the threshold; under the default scheme it also
reports the largest value reached and where. It remains a test aid and not an implementation
of MeTTaDinC.

The simulation. python3 models/wx_sim.py runs the eight weeks from the seed 20261104,
prints the alarm and its confirmation, and writes the twelve streams with their digests into the
directory given as its argument, by default streams_regenerated/ beside streams/, so that
the shipped streams are not overwritten. python3 make_module.py regenerates the shipped
streams in place and writes wmtm_example.metta from the template with their digests; on an
unchanged bundle it changes nothing. Every random draw of an item is made when the item is
created, so the run does not depend on the order in which later quantities are computed. The
ground truth stays inside the simulation and reaches the streams only as the review’s labels,
the audit’s findings and the column truth of the journal, which the record designs never read.
